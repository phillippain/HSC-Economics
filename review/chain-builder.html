The RBA raises the cash rate

Contractionary monetary policy · dot points 4D.3, 4D.2, 4I.3, 3C.3 · reverse: The RBA cuts the cash rate

Trigger: The RBA Monetary Policy Board raises its cash rate target to bring inflation back to the 2–3% target band.

Start of the chain

  1. Cash rate ▲ rises: The Board lifts its target for the interest rate on overnight loans between banks.
  2. Market interest rates on loans and deposits ▲ rises: Banks' funding costs rise, so they pass the increase on to mortgages, business loans and savings accounts.

Strand: Saving and spending

  1. Cost of borrowing and reward for saving ▲ rises: Credit is dearer and saving earns more, so households and firms have a reason to spend less now.
  2. Household consumption ▼ falls: Households put off spending, especially on items bought with credit such as cars and renovations.
  3. Business investment ▼ falls: Fewer projects earn enough to cover the higher cost of borrowing.

Strand: Cash flow

  1. Repayments on variable-rate mortgages ▲ rises: Lenders raise the rates on existing loans, so required repayments increase.
  2. Disposable income of borrowers ▼ falls: More of each pay goes to the bank. Savers earn more interest, but households in total owe more than they hold in deposits.
  3. Household consumption ▼ falls: With less left over after repayments, indebted households cut back.

Strand: Asset prices and wealth

  1. Demand for housing and shares ▼ falls: Buyers can borrow less, and safer interest-bearing assets now look more attractive.
  2. House and share prices ▼ falls: Weaker demand lowers asset prices, or slows their growth.
  3. Household consumption ▼ falls: Households that feel less wealthy spend less: the wealth effect.

Strand: Exchange rate

  1. Return on Australian assets compared with overseas ▲ rises: Australian interest rates rise relative to those in other countries.
  2. Demand for Australian dollars ▲ rises: Foreign investors buy Australian dollars to earn the higher return.
  3. Value of the Australian dollar ▲ rises: More demand for the currency causes an appreciation.
  4. Net exports ▼ falls: Exports become less competitive and imports cheaper. Cheaper imports also lower inflation directly.

End of the chain

  1. Aggregate demand ▼ falls: Consumption, investment and net exports are all weaker.
  2. Economic growth ▼ falls: Firms cut production as spending slows.
  3. Unemployment ▲ rises: Firms need fewer workers, so spare capacity opens up in the labour market.
  4. Inflation ▼ falls: Weaker demand and slower wages growth give firms less room to raise prices.

How long it takes: Interest rates change quickly, but the full effect on inflation can take one to two years.

Where the chain can break

  • It works on demand. It cannot remove inflation that comes from supply shocks, such as higher oil prices.
  • Fixed-rate loans and savings buffers delay the cash flow channel.
  • It is a blunt tool: borrowers and interest-sensitive industries such as housing bear most of the cost.
  • The exchange rate channel depends on what other central banks do with their rates.
  • If people expect high inflation to continue, wages and prices keep rising for longer.

Diagram: AD–AS diagram: AD shifts left from AD to AD₁, so the price level and real GDP both fall.

The government runs an expansionary budget

Expansionary fiscal policy · dot points 4C.2, 4B.1, 3A.1, 3A.3, 4I.1, 4I.2 · reverse: The government runs a contractionary budget

Trigger: The Australian Government raises its spending or cuts taxes to lift economic activity.

Start of the chain

  1. Budget deficit ▲ rises: Discretionary decisions raise spending or lower revenue, so the budget moves towards deficit.

Strand: Government spending

  1. Government spending (G) ▲ rises: Spending on infrastructure, services or payments is an injection into the circular flow.
  2. Output and employment in supplying firms ▲ rises: Firms that win the work produce more and hire more workers.

Strand: Tax cuts and transfers

  1. Household disposable income ▲ rises: Households keep more of their pay or receive larger payments.
  2. Household consumption (C) ▲ rises: Part of the extra income is spent and part is saved. The share spent is the marginal propensity to consume.

End of the chain

  1. Aggregate demand ▲ rises: G and C are both components of AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  2. Further rounds of spending ▲ rises: One person's spending is another's income, so the first increase is multiplied: k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  3. Economic growth ▲ rises: Firms raise production to meet the extra demand.
  4. Cyclical unemployment ▼ falls: Demand for labour is derived from demand for goods and services.
  5. Inflationary pressure ▲ rises: If the economy is near full capacity, extra demand pulls prices up.

How long it takes: There is an implementation lag: budgets are annual and most measures need legislation. Once the money is spent, the effect is fairly fast.

Where the chain can break

  • Some of the extra income leaks into saving, tax and imports, which weakens the multiplier and widens the current account deficit.
  • Government borrowing can push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • Deficits add to public debt, which limits what future budgets can do.
  • When inflation is high, stimulus works against the RBA's monetary policy.
  • Political constraints: spending is easy to start and hard to withdraw.

Diagram: AD–AS diagram: AD shifts right from AD to AD₁, so real GDP and the price level both rise.

The Australian dollar depreciates

A fall in the exchange rate · dot points 2C.6, 2C.3, 3D.3, 3C.3 · reverse: The Australian dollar appreciates

Trigger: The value of the Australian dollar falls against other currencies.

Start of the chain

    Strand: Exports

    1. Price of Australian exports in foreign currency ▼ falls: Overseas buyers need less of their own currency to buy the same Australian goods and services.
    2. International competitiveness ▲ rises: Australian goods and services are cheaper than rival products.
    3. Export volumes ▲ rises: Overseas buyers purchase more, and tourists and students find Australia cheaper.
    4. Net exports and aggregate demand ▲ rises: Exports are an injection, so growth and employment rise.

    Strand: Imports

    1. Price of imports in Australian dollars ▲ rises: Each Australian dollar buys less foreign currency.
    2. Import volumes ▼ falls: Households and firms switch to local substitutes where they can.
    3. Output of import-competing firms ▲ rises: Local producers win sales from dearer imports.

    Strand: Inflation

    1. Cost of imported goods and inputs ▲ rises: Fuel, machinery and consumer goods priced in foreign currency cost more in Australian dollars.
    2. Imported inflation ▲ rises: Firms pass higher import costs on to consumers, raising the CPI.
    3. Real incomes ▼ falls: Wages buy less when prices rise faster than pay.

    Strand: Foreign debt

    1. Australian-dollar value of debt owed in foreign currencies ▲ rises: The same foreign-currency debt converts into more Australian dollars: the valuation effect.
    2. Cost of servicing foreign debt ▲ rises: Interest payments in foreign currency take more Australian dollars.
    3. Net primary income deficit ▲ rises: Larger interest payments are debits on the current account.

    Overall: A depreciation helps exporters and import-competing firms but raises inflation and the cost of foreign debt.

    How long it takes: Volumes respond slowly. The current account can worsen at first because import bills rise before export volumes do: the J-curve.

    Where the chain can break

    • The gain depends on how responsive buyers are to price. Commodity exports are priced in US dollars and limited by supply.
    • Firms that rely on imported inputs face higher costs, which offsets their competitive gain.
    • If the depreciation was caused by lower commodity prices, the loss of export income can outweigh the benefit.
    • Higher imported inflation may lead the RBA to raise interest rates.

    Diagram: Market for the Australian dollar: the supply of A$ shifts right (or demand shifts left), so the exchange rate falls from ER to ER₁.

    Commodity prices fall

    A fall in the terms of trade · dot points 2A.1, 2C.2, 3D.3, 4H.2, 3A.5 · reverse: Commodity prices rise

    Trigger: World prices for Australia's main exports, such as iron ore, coal and gas, fall.

    Start of the chain

    1. Terms of trade ▼ falls: Export prices fall relative to import prices, so a given volume of exports buys fewer imports.

    Strand: Balance of payments

    1. Export income ▼ falls: The same volume of exports earns less.
    2. Balance on goods and services ▼ falls: Export credits fall while import debits are unchanged.
    3. Current account balance ▼ falls: A smaller trade surplus moves the current account towards deficit.

    Strand: Exchange rate

    1. Demand for Australian dollars ▼ falls: Overseas buyers need fewer Australian dollars to pay for exports.
    2. Value of the Australian dollar ▼ falls: Lower demand for the currency causes a depreciation.
    3. Competitiveness of other exports ▲ rises: The lower dollar helps farmers, manufacturers, tourism and education. The floating exchange rate acts as a shock absorber.

    Strand: Income and the budget

    1. Mining profits and national income ▼ falls: Resource companies earn less for each tonne sold.
    2. Mining investment and jobs ▼ falls: Lower prices make new projects less profitable.
    3. Company tax revenue ▼ falls: Lower profits mean less tax collected.
    4. Budget balance ▼ falls: Revenue falls short of forecasts, so the deficit widens or the surplus shrinks.

    End of the chain

    1. Growth in real national income ▼ falls: Australia earns less from the rest of the world for the same work, so living standards grow more slowly.

    How long it takes: The exchange rate reacts within days. Investment, jobs and the budget take months to years.

    Where the chain can break

    • The depreciation offsets part of the income loss.
    • Export volumes may rise as earlier mining investment comes into production.
    • The size of the effect depends on how much of Australia's export base is commodities.
    • Global influences are outside the control of Australian policy.

    Diagram: Market for the Australian dollar: demand for A$ shifts left, so the exchange rate falls from ER to ER₁.

    Australia cuts its tariffs

    Trade liberalisation · dot points 2D.3, 1C.2, 2D.1, 4E.2, 3B.3 · reverse: Australia raises its tariffs

    Trigger: The Australian Government lowers or removes tariffs on imported goods.

    Start of the chain

    1. Price of imports in Australia ▼ falls: The tax on imports is lower, so the landed price falls towards the world price.

    Strand: Consumers and firms that use imports

    1. Prices paid by consumers ▼ falls: Imports are cheaper, and local firms must match them.
    2. Input costs for firms ▼ falls: Imported machinery and components cost less.
    3. Real incomes ▲ rises: The same income buys more, and inflation is lower.

    Strand: Protected industries

    1. Competition from imports ▲ rises: Local producers lose the price advantage the tariff gave them.
    2. Output of less efficient local firms ▼ falls: Firms that cannot match the world price lose sales or close.
    3. Structural unemployment ▲ rises: Workers in those industries and regions lose jobs, and their skills may not match the jobs available.

    Strand: Resources and efficiency

    1. Resources moving to industries with a comparative advantage ▲ rises: Labour and capital shift to where Australia produces at a lower opportunity cost.
    2. Productivity and international competitiveness ▲ rises: Competition forces firms to cut costs and innovate.
    3. Exports ▲ rises: More efficient firms with cheaper inputs can sell more overseas.

    Strand: Government

    1. Tariff revenue ▼ falls: A lower rate is collected on each import.

    Overall: The costs are immediate and fall on particular industries and regions. The gains are spread widely and build over time.

    How long it takes: Prices fall quickly. The shift of resources into more efficient industries takes years.

    Where the chain can break

    • Adjustment costs are concentrated, so displaced workers need retraining and support.
    • Other countries may keep their own protection, so Australian exporters do not gain equal access.
    • Some industries may be kept for strategic or security reasons.
    • Political constraints: the workers who lose are easy to see and the consumers who gain are not.

    Diagram: Tariff diagram: the price falls from Pw+t to Pw. Domestic output falls, consumption rises and imports increase.

    Wages grow faster than productivity

    Wage pressure · dot points 3C.3, 4F.4, 3B.3, 4A.7 · reverse: Wages grow more slowly than productivity

    Trigger: Nominal wages rise faster than output per worker.

    Start of the chain

      Strand: Costs and prices

      1. Unit labour costs ▲ rises: Firms pay more for each unit of output because pay has risen faster than output per worker.
      2. Prices ▲ rises: Firms raise prices to protect their profit margins.
      3. Cost-push inflation ▲ rises: Higher costs of production shift aggregate supply to the left.
      4. Inflationary expectations ▲ rises: Workers expect higher prices and seek higher pay again: the wage–price spiral.

      Strand: Demand

      1. Household incomes ▲ rises: Workers take home more pay.
      2. Household consumption ▲ rises: Most of the extra income is spent.
      3. Demand-pull inflation ▲ rises: If the economy is near capacity, the extra spending pulls prices up.

      Strand: Jobs and competitiveness

      1. Cost of labour compared with capital ▲ rises: Workers become more expensive relative to machines and software.
      2. Demand for labour ▼ falls: Firms substitute capital for labour or hire fewer workers.
      3. International competitiveness ▼ falls: Australian costs rise relative to those of trading partners.
      4. Unemployment ▲ rises: Fewer jobs are offered at the higher wage, especially for low-skilled workers.

      Overall: Wage rises are sustainable when matched by productivity. When they are not, they add to inflation and can cost jobs.

      How long it takes: Wage decisions flow through over one to two years as awards and enterprise agreements are renewed.

      Where the chain can break

      • If productivity grows as fast as wages, unit labour costs do not rise.
      • Firms may absorb higher wages in lower profit margins instead of raising prices.
      • After a period of falling real wages, a rise may only restore lost purchasing power.
      • The RBA may raise the cash rate if wages growth threatens the inflation target.

      Diagram: AD–AS diagram: AS shifts left from AS to AS₁, so the price level rises and real GDP falls.

      Growth slows in Australia's major trading partners

      A global downturn · dot points 1A.8, 1D.9, 4H.2, 3A.7, 2A.1 · reverse: Growth picks up in Australia's major trading partners

      Trigger: Economic growth falls in economies such as China, Japan and the United States.

      Start of the chain

        Strand: Trade

        1. Overseas demand for Australian exports ▼ falls: Trading partners with lower incomes buy fewer resources, services and manufactured goods.
        2. Export volumes and commodity prices ▼ falls: Weaker world demand lowers both the quantity sold and its price.
        3. Export income ▼ falls: Lower prices and volumes cut what exporters earn.

        Strand: Confidence and investment

        1. Business and consumer confidence ▼ falls: Bad news from overseas and falling share prices make firms and households cautious.
        2. Investment and consumption ▼ falls: Firms delay projects and households save more.

        Strand: Financial flows

        1. Investors' willingness to take risks ▼ falls: In a downturn investors move funds to safe assets such as US government bonds.
        2. Value of the Australian dollar ▼ falls: Less capital inflow and lower commodity prices reduce demand for the currency.
        3. Competitiveness of Australian exports ▲ rises: The lower dollar cushions exporters and import-competing firms.

        End of the chain

        1. Aggregate demand ▼ falls: Exports, investment and consumption are all weaker.
        2. Economic growth ▼ falls: The Australian business cycle follows the international business cycle.
        3. Cyclical unemployment ▲ rises: Firms need fewer workers when demand falls.
        4. Budget deficit ▲ rises: Automatic stabilisers: tax revenue falls and welfare payments rise without any new decision.

        How long it takes: Share prices and the dollar react at once. Exports, jobs and the budget follow over the next few quarters.

        Where the chain can break

        • Expansionary monetary and fiscal policy can offset part of the fall in demand.
        • The floating exchange rate absorbs some of the shock.
        • The effect depends on which trading partner slows: China matters most for resource exports.
        • Global influences limit what domestic policy can achieve.

        Diagram: AD–AS diagram: AD shifts left. Or the business cycle: Australia moving from an upswing into a downswing.

        Productivity rises

        Successful microeconomic reform · dot points 4E.1, 4E.2, 3A.6, 4I.1, 4I.3 · reverse: Productivity falls

        Trigger: Microeconomic reform, new technology or better skills raise output per unit of input.

        Start of the chain

        1. Unit costs of production ▼ falls: Firms produce more from the same labour and capital.

        Strand: Prices

        1. Prices charged by firms ▼ falls: Competition pushes firms to pass lower costs on to buyers.
        2. Inflation ▼ falls: The economy can grow without the same pressure on prices.

        Strand: Competitiveness

        1. International competitiveness ▲ rises: Australian goods and services become cheaper relative to rivals.
        2. Exports and import-competing output ▲ rises: Local firms win sales at home and overseas.
        3. Current account deficit ▼ falls: A stronger trade balance improves the current account.

        Strand: Incomes

        1. Firms' capacity to pay higher wages ▲ rises: Each worker produces more, so higher pay does not raise unit labour costs.
        2. Real incomes and living standards ▲ rises: Wages can rise faster than prices.

        End of the chain

        1. Aggregate supply ▲ rises: The economy's productive capacity grows: the AS curve shifts right.
        2. Sustainable rate of economic growth ▲ rises: The economy can grow faster without causing inflation.

        How long it takes: Long. The benefits of reform can take many years to appear.

        Where the chain can break

        • Time lags: governments bear the political cost long before the gains arrive.
        • Reform causes structural unemployment in the short run as inefficient firms close.
        • The gains may go to profits instead of wages or lower prices if markets are not competitive.
        • Political constraints: the groups that lose from reform resist it.

        Diagram: AD–AS diagram: AS shifts right from AS to AS₁, so real GDP rises and the price level falls.

        Inflation rises above the target band

        The effects of high inflation · dot points 3C.4, 4A.3, 4A.7, 3C.3 · reverse: none

        Trigger: The general level of prices rises faster than the RBA's 2–3% target.

        Start of the chain

          Strand: Households

          1. Prices compared with wages ▲ rises: Prices rise faster than most pay packets.
          2. Real wages and purchasing power ▼ falls: The same income buys fewer goods and services.
          3. Inequality ▲ rises: People on low or fixed incomes spend most of their income on essentials and are hit hardest.

          Strand: Savers and borrowers

          1. Real value of savings ▼ falls: Money in the bank buys less each year unless interest rates keep up.
          2. Real value of existing debts ▼ falls: Borrowers repay with dollars that are worth less, so wealth shifts from lenders to borrowers.

          Strand: Competitiveness

          1. Australian prices compared with trading partners ▲ rises: Local costs and prices rise faster than those overseas.
          2. International competitiveness ▼ falls: Exports are dearer and imports relatively cheaper.
          3. Net exports ▼ falls: Export volumes fall and import volumes rise.

          Strand: Expectations and policy

          1. Inflationary expectations ▲ rises: People come to expect that prices will keep rising.
          2. Wage claims ▲ rises: Workers seek pay rises to cover expected inflation.
          3. Cash rate ▲ rises: The RBA tightens monetary policy to return inflation to target.
          4. Economic growth ▼ falls: Higher interest rates slow spending, and unemployment rises.

          Overall: High inflation redistributes income, erodes competitiveness and forces a policy response that slows growth.

          How long it takes: Real incomes fall straight away. The policy response and its effect on growth follow over one to two years.

          Where the chain can break

          • The effect on households depends on whether wages and welfare payments are indexed to prices.
          • Borrowers gain only if interest rates do not rise by as much as inflation.
          • If trading partners have similar inflation, competitiveness is little changed.
          • Low, stable inflation is the aim. Deflation carries costs of its own.

          Diagram: Phillips curve, or AD–AS showing the cause: AD shifting right (demand-pull) or AS shifting left (cost-push).

          Australia runs a current account deficit year after year

          External stability · dot points 3D.3, 2B.3, 3D.1, 4A.4 · reverse: none

          Trigger: Payments to the rest of the world on the current account exceed receipts for many years.

          Start of the chain

          1. Financial account surplus ▲ rises: A current account deficit must be funded by borrowing from overseas or selling assets to foreign investors.

          Strand: Debt

          1. Net foreign debt ▲ rises: Each year's borrowing adds to the stock of debt.
          2. Interest payments to overseas lenders ▲ rises: A larger debt costs more to service.
          3. Net primary income deficit ▲ rises: Interest payments are debits on the current account.
          4. Current account deficit ▲ rises: The deficit feeds itself: the debt trap.

          Strand: Equity

          1. Foreign ownership of Australian assets ▲ rises: Foreign investors buy shares, companies and property.
          2. Profits and dividends paid overseas ▲ rises: Owners take their returns out of Australia.
          3. Net primary income deficit ▲ rises: Dividends are also debits on the current account.

          Strand: Vulnerability

          1. Reliance on foreign lenders ▲ rises: Australia needs a steady inflow of funds to cover the gap.
          2. Exposure to changes in investor confidence ▲ rises: If lenders lose confidence they demand higher returns or withdraw funds.
          3. Risk of a sharp depreciation and higher interest rates ▲ rises: A sudden fall in capital inflow lowers the dollar and raises the cost of borrowing.

          Strand: The other view

          1. Funds available for investment ▲ rises: Foreign saving lets Australia invest more than it saves.
          2. Productive capacity ▲ rises: Investment in mines, infrastructure and technology expands what the economy can produce.
          3. Future export income ▲ rises: The extra output can service the debt. On this view a deficit from private decisions is not a problem: the Pitchford thesis.

          Overall: A current account deficit is sustainable when the borrowing funds productive investment and lenders stay confident.

          How long it takes: The stock of liabilities builds slowly, but a loss of confidence can happen within days.

          Where the chain can break

          • Most foreign debt is private, taken on by firms and banks that expect to repay it.
          • Much of the debt is hedged or in Australian dollars, which reduces the valuation risk.
          • A floating exchange rate adjusts gradually, which lowers the chance of a crisis.
          • The debt servicing ratio shows whether export income can cover the interest.

          Diagram: Flow chart of the debt trap cycle: CAD → foreign borrowing → net foreign debt → interest payments → net primary income deficit → CAD.

          A price is put on carbon emissions

          Market-based environmental policy · dot points 4G.2, 3F.2, 4I.6, 3F.4 · reverse: none

          Trigger: The government makes polluters pay for each tonne of greenhouse gas they emit, through a tax or tradable permits.

          Start of the chain

          1. Private cost of emissions-intensive production ▲ rises: The external cost of pollution is added to the firm's own costs: the externality is internalised.

          Strand: Producers

          1. Supply of emissions-intensive goods ▼ falls: Higher costs shift the supply curve to the left.
          2. Price of those goods ▲ rises: Firms pass on part of the cost.
          3. Quantity produced and consumed ▼ falls: Buyers respond to the higher price.
          4. Emissions ▼ falls: Less of the polluting good is made.

          Strand: Switching

          1. Cost of clean technology compared with polluting technology ▼ falls: Renewables and energy efficiency become relatively cheaper.
          2. Investment in clean technology ▲ rises: Firms have a lasting incentive to cut the emissions they must pay for.
          3. Emissions over time ▼ falls: Production methods change, not only the quantity produced.

          Strand: Households

          1. Energy prices ▲ rises: Electricity and gas producers pass on the carbon cost.
          2. Cost of living ▲ rises: Low-income households spend a larger share of income on energy, so the effect is regressive.

          Strand: Trade-exposed firms

          1. Costs compared with overseas rivals that face no carbon price ▲ rises: Australian producers pay a cost their competitors do not.
          2. International competitiveness ▼ falls: Exports and import-competing firms lose sales.
          3. Risk of production moving offshore ▲ rises: Emissions shift to another country instead of falling: carbon leakage.

          Overall: A carbon price cuts emissions at the lowest cost to the economy, but raises prices and needs compensation and international co-operation.

          How long it takes: Prices change at once. Investment in new technology takes years.

          Where the chain can break

          • Political constraints: higher energy prices are unpopular, and policy has changed with governments.
          • Carbon leakage, unless other countries act or trade-exposed firms are shielded.
          • Demand for energy is not very responsive to price in the short run.
          • Climate change is a global problem, so one country's action has limited effect alone.

          Diagram: Negative externality diagram: the carbon price lifts the private cost curve (MPC) up to the social cost curve (MSC), so output falls to the socially efficient level.

          Unemployment rises

          The effects of unemployment · dot points 3B.6, 4A.2, 3E.6, 4C.1 · reverse: none

          Trigger: The unemployment rate increases as the economy slows.

          Start of the chain

            Strand: Lost output

            1. Number of people producing goods and services ▼ falls: Workers who want jobs are idle.
            2. Real GDP compared with its potential ▼ falls: The economy operates inside its production possibility frontier. The lost output is an opportunity cost.

            Strand: Households and demand

            1. Household incomes ▼ falls: Wages are replaced by much lower welfare payments.
            2. Household consumption ▼ falls: Unemployed households spend less, and employed households save more in case they are next.
            3. Aggregate demand ▼ falls: Lower spending leads firms to cut more jobs: a downward multiplier.

            Strand: The budget

            1. Income tax revenue ▼ falls: Fewer people earn taxable income.
            2. Welfare payments ▲ rises: More people qualify for JobSeeker.
            3. Budget deficit ▲ rises: These automatic stabilisers widen the deficit without any new decision.

            Strand: Skills and inequality

            1. Long-term unemployment ▲ rises: The longer people are out of work, the harder it is to return.
            2. Skills of the unemployed ▼ falls: Skills and work habits fade, so cyclical unemployment can become structural.
            3. Inequality and social costs ▲ rises: Income losses fall on particular groups and regions, with poorer health and family stress.

            Strand: Wages and prices

            1. Spare capacity in the labour market ▲ rises: More people compete for each job.
            2. Wages growth ▼ falls: Workers have less bargaining power.
            3. Inflation ▼ falls: Slower growth in labour costs and weaker demand ease price pressure.

            Overall: Unemployment wastes resources, widens the budget deficit and inequality, and eases inflation.

            How long it takes: Jobs are lost quickly in a downturn. Long-term unemployment and skill loss last for years after growth returns.

            Where the chain can break

            • The costs depend on how long people stay unemployed, not only on the unemployment rate.
            • Underemployment and hidden unemployment mean the rate understates the spare capacity.
            • Welfare payments and automatic stabilisers cushion the fall in consumption.
            • Below the NAIRU, lower unemployment adds to inflation, which is a conflict between objectives.

            Diagram: AD–AS diagram: AD shifts left and real GDP falls below full-employment output. Or a production possibility frontier with a point inside it.
