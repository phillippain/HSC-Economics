The four systems

Floating: The market sets the rate

Who sets the rate
Demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, minute by minute.
What the central bank does
Normally nothing. It keeps reserves but does not use them to steer the rate.
Reserves
Small: reserves are not needed to defend a rate.
Monetary policy
Full independence: the central bank can set interest rates for domestic goals such as inflation.
Examples
Australia since December 1983; the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom, the euro area.
Advantages
  • The rate adjusts by itself to shocks, acting as a shock absorber for the economy
  • Monetary policy is free to target inflation and employment
  • No need to hold large reserves; speculators have no fixed rate to attack
Disadvantages
  • The rate can be volatile, creating uncertainty for exporters, importers and borrowers
  • A falling currency raises import prices and the cost of foreign-currency debt
  • The rate can overshoot what the fundamentals justify

Managed float: The market sets the rate, the central bank smooths it

Who sets the rate
Mostly the market, but the central bank buys or sells its currency to slow large or disorderly moves. Also called a dirty float.
What the central bank does
Leans against the wind: buys its own currency when it is falling fast and sells it when it is rising fast.
Reserves
Moderate: enough to intervene from time to time.
Monetary policy
Mostly independent, although heavy intervention affects the money supply unless it is offset (sterilised).
Examples
Many emerging economies, such as India and Indonesia. The Reserve Bank of Australia has intervened occasionally, for example when the market became disorderly in 2008.
Advantages
  • Keeps most of the shock-absorbing benefit of a float
  • Reduces sharp swings that damage confidence and trade
  • Buys time for exporters, importers and borrowers to adjust
Disadvantages
  • Intervention only delays an adjustment that the fundamentals require
  • Uses up reserves if the central bank fights a persistent trend
  • It can be unclear what the central bank is aiming for

Flexible peg: A fixed rate that the authorities reset when pressure builds

Who sets the rate
The government or central bank announces a rate (the peg) and defends it, but changes the peg when it is clearly out of line with the market.
What the central bank does
Buys or sells its currency every day to hold the peg, then devalues or revalues to a new peg when the gap becomes too large.
Reserves
Large: needed to hold the peg between adjustments.
Monetary policy
Limited: defending the peg changes the money supply, and interest rates may have to be used to protect the currency.
Examples
Australia before the float: pegged to the US dollar from 1971, to a trade-weighted basket from 1974, and adjusted daily (a crawling peg) from 1976 to 1983.
Advantages
  • More certainty for trade and investment than a float, most of the time
  • The peg can be moved, so a serious misalignment does not have to last
  • Less risk of running out of reserves than a rigid fixed rate
Disadvantages
  • Adjustments come as sudden jumps (devaluations or revaluations) rather than gradual moves
  • Speculators can profit by betting on the next adjustment, which adds to the pressure
  • Between adjustments the economy carries the cost of the wrong exchange rate

Fixed: The authorities hold one rate whatever happens

Who sets the rate
The government or central bank sets the rate and commits to keeping it.
What the central bank does
Buys or sells whatever amount of its currency is needed to hold the rate, for as long as it has the reserves to do so.
Reserves
Very large: the rate lasts only as long as the reserves do.
Monetary policy
Almost none: the money supply and interest rates must follow whatever is needed to hold the rate.
Examples
Hong Kong (to the US dollar since 1983), Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states (to the US dollar), Denmark (to the euro).
Advantages
  • Certainty for exporters, importers and investors
  • Can anchor inflation if the currency is fixed to a low-inflation currency
  • Removes exchange rate speculation while the rate is credible
Disadvantages
  • The rate cannot absorb shocks, so output and jobs adjust instead
  • Monetary policy cannot be used for domestic goals
  • If reserves run out the rate collapses in a currency crisis

Shocks

  • Commodity prices rise (demand +8, supply +0): Foreign buyers need more Australian dollars to pay for dearer exports, so demand for the dollar increases.
  • Global downturn (demand -8, supply +0): Trading partners buy fewer Australian exports, so demand for the dollar decreases.
  • Australian interest rates rise relative to overseas (demand +5, supply -3): Foreign savers buy Australian dollars to earn the higher return (demand increases) and Australians keep more funds at home (supply decreases).
  • Overseas interest rates rise relative to Australia (demand -5, supply +3): Funds move overseas for the higher return: demand for the dollar decreases and supply increases.
  • Australians buy more imports (demand +0, supply +7): Importers sell Australian dollars to buy foreign currency, so the supply of the dollar increases.
  • Speculators expect the dollar to fall (demand -4, supply +6): Speculators sell Australian dollars now and buyers hold off, so supply increases and demand decreases.
  • Foreign investment boom (demand +7, supply +0): Foreign firms buy Australian dollars to invest in Australia, so demand for the dollar increases.
  • Higher inflation in Australia than overseas (demand -4, supply +4): Australian goods become less competitive: exports fall (demand decreases) and imports rise (supply increases).
  • Let a period pass with no new shock (demand +0, supply +0): Nothing new happens. Watch what continues to change under each system.

Australia's exchange rate history

  • To 1971: Fixed to the British pound. The Australian currency was fixed to sterling, and through it to the US dollar under the Bretton Woods system of fixed rates.
  • 1971: Pegged to the US dollar. As Bretton Woods broke down, the peg was moved from sterling to the US dollar.
  • 1974: Pegged to a trade-weighted basket. The dollar was pegged to a basket of trading partners' currencies (the origin of the Trade Weighted Index) rather than to one currency.
  • 1976: Crawling peg. After a large devaluation in November 1976, the peg was reviewed and adjusted frequently by a small group of officials. This was a flexible peg.
  • December 1983: The float. With large capital flows making the peg impossible to manage, the government floated the dollar and removed most exchange controls.
  • Since 1983: Floating, with occasional intervention. The market sets the rate. The Reserve Bank has intervened at times when the market was disorderly, for example in 2008, but does not target a level.
