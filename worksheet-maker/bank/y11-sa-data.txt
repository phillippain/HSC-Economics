=== sa y11-sd-P1c-ppf
c: P1c.4 P1c.1 P1c.5
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The production possibilities schedule shows the combinations of cars and wheat an economy can produce when it uses all its resources efficiently.
TABLE: Production possibilities schedule
| Point | Cars ('000) | Wheat ('000 tonnes) |
| A | 0 | 200 |
| B | 10 | 190 |
| C | 20 | 170 |
| D | 30 | 140 |
| E | 40 | 100 |
| F | 50 | 40 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the opportunity cost of increasing car production from 20,000 to 30,000.
ANS: Wheat falls from 170,000 to 140,000 tonnes, so the 10,000 extra cars cost 30,000 tonnes of wheat (3 tonnes per car).
PART (b) 2: Explain why the opportunity cost of producing cars rises as more cars are produced.
PTS:
- Each extra 10,000 cars costs more wheat: 10, 20, 30, 40 and then 60 thousand tonnes (1)
- Resources are not equally suited to both goods; as car output rises, resources better suited to growing wheat are moved into cars, so more wheat is given up for each car (1)
PART (c) 2: The economy is producing 20,000 cars and 120,000 tonnes of wheat. Explain what this shows.
PTS:
- This point is inside the frontier: with 20,000 cars the economy could produce 170,000 tonnes of wheat (1)
- Resources are unemployed or underemployed (or used inefficiently), so 50,000 tonnes of wheat could be gained at no opportunity cost (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P1d-flow
c: P1d.4 P1d.5 P1d.2
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows the flows in the circular flow of income for an economy over one year.
TABLE: Circular flow of income ($ billion)
| Flow | $ billion |
| Savings (S) | 120 |
| Taxation (T) | 160 |
| Imports (M) | 110 |
| Investment (I) | 140 |
| Government spending (G) | 150 |
| Exports (X) | 90 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate total leakages and total injections.
ANS: Leakages S + T + M = 120 + 160 + 110 = $390 billion. Injections I + G + X = 140 + 150 + 90 = $380 billion.
PART (b) 2: Explain what will happen to national income in this economy.
PTS:
- Leakages exceed injections by $10 billion, so less is spent than is received as income (1)
- Spending, production and income fall until leakages again equal injections (equilibrium) (1)
PART (c) 2: Explain ONE way the government could keep national income at its current level.
PTS:
- Identifies a suitable change, e.g. increase government spending (G) or reduce taxation (T) by $10 billion (1)
- Explains it: injections rise or leakages fall until S + T + M = I + G + X at the current level of income (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P1e-gdp
c: P1e.4 P1e.5 P1e.7
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows quarterly growth in real GDP and the unemployment rate for an economy.
TABLE: Real GDP growth and unemployment
| Quarter | Real GDP growth (%) | Unemployment rate (%) |
| 1 | 0.9 | 4.0 |
| 2 | 0.7 | 4.0 |
| 3 | 0.2 | 4.2 |
| 4 | −0.3 | 4.6 |
| 5 | −0.5 | 5.1 |
| 6 | 0.1 | 5.4 |
| 7 | 0.6 | 5.3 |
PART (a) 1: Identify the two consecutive quarters in which real GDP fell.
ANS: Quarters 4 and 5 (growth of −0.3 and −0.5 per cent).
PART (b) 2: Identify the stage of the business cycle in Quarters 4 and 5, and the trough.
ANS: Quarters 4 and 5 are a contraction (downturn), the two quarters of falling GDP often called a recession. The trough is around Quarter 5 to 6, after which growth recovers.
PART (c) 3: Explain why unemployment kept rising in Quarter 6, even though GDP had begun to grow again.
PTS:
- Unemployment lags the business cycle: firms wait to be confident the recovery will last before hiring (1)
- Growth of 0.1 per cent is too weak to create enough jobs for the growing labour force (1)
- Firms first use their existing workers more fully (more hours) before taking on new staff (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P2b-movies
c: P2b.3 P2b.4 P2b.5
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows the weekly demand schedule for tickets at a cinema.
TABLE: Demand for cinema tickets
| Price ($) | Tickets demanded per week |
| 10 | 800 |
| 12 | 700 |
| 14 | 600 |
| 16 | 500 |
| 18 | 400 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the change in the quantity of tickets demanded when the price rises from $12 to $16.
ANS: The quantity demanded falls from 700 to 500 tickets a week, a contraction of 200 tickets.
PART (b) 2: Distinguish between a contraction of demand and a decrease in demand, using this cinema as an example.
PTS:
- A contraction of demand is a movement along the demand curve caused by a rise in the good's own price, e.g. the rise from $12 to $16 (1)
- A decrease in demand is a shift of the whole curve to the left caused by a non-price factor, e.g. a fall in incomes, so fewer tickets are demanded at every price (1)
PART (c) 2: Streaming services raise their subscription prices sharply. Explain the likely effect on the demand for cinema tickets.
PTS:
- Streaming and cinema are substitutes (1)
- Some viewers switch to the cinema, so demand for tickets increases: more tickets are demanded at each price and the demand curve shifts right (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P2d-market
c: P2d.1 P2d.2 P2b.5 P2c.5
lv: 2
k: calc table diagram
LEAD: The table shows the market for a good.
TABLE: Market schedule
| Price ($) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
| 2 | 1,000 | 400 |
| 3 | 900 | 600 |
| 4 | 800 | 800 |
| 5 | 700 | 1,000 |
| 6 | 600 | 1,200 |
PART (a) 1: Identify the equilibrium price and quantity.
ANS: $4 and 800 units, where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
PART (b) 2: Calculate the surplus or shortage at a price of $5, and explain what will happen to the price.
ANS: At $5 quantity supplied (1,000) exceeds quantity demanded (700): a surplus of 300 units. Sellers cut prices to clear unsold stock, so the price falls back towards $4.
PART (c) 2: Demand increases by 300 units at every price. Identify the new equilibrium price and quantity.
ANS: The new quantities demanded are 1,300, 1,200, 1,100, 1,000 and 900. At $5, quantity demanded (1,000) equals quantity supplied (1,000), so the new equilibrium is $5 and 1,000 units: both price and quantity rise.

=== sa y11-sd-P2e-cinema
c: P2e.3 P2e.5 P2e.4
lv: 2
k: calc explain
LEAD: A cinema raises the price of a ticket from $20 to $22. Its weekly ticket sales fall from 2,000 to 1,700.
PART (a) 2: Calculate the price elasticity of demand using the percentage method.
ANS: % change in quantity = (1,700 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = −15%. % change in price = (22 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 10%. PED = −15 ÷ 10 = −1.5 (1.5): demand is relatively elastic.
PART (b) 2: Calculate the change in the cinema's weekly revenue, and explain it using the price elasticity of demand.
ANS: Revenue falls from $40,000 (20 × 2,000) to $37,400 (22 × 1,700), down $2,600. Because demand is elastic, the percentage fall in tickets sold (15%) is larger than the percentage rise in price (10%).
PART (c) 2: Outline ONE reason why the demand for cinema tickets may be price elastic.
PTS:
- Identifies a reason, e.g. close substitutes such as streaming and other entertainment; not a necessity (1)
- Outlines it: when the price rises, many customers switch to the substitute or go less often (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P2e-tr
c: P2e.3 P2e.1 P2e.5
lv: 3
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows what happens to the quantity demanded of three goods when each one's price rises by 20 per cent.
TABLE: Responses to a 20 per cent price rise
| Good | Change in quantity demanded |
| Petrol | −4% |
| Restaurant meals | −30% |
| Bus fares (one city) | −20% |
PART (a) 2: Calculate the price elasticity of demand for each good.
ANS: Petrol: 4 ÷ 20 = 0.2 (inelastic). Restaurant meals: 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 (elastic). Bus fares: 20 ÷ 20 = 1 (unit elastic).
PART (b) 2: Explain what happens to the total revenue of petrol sellers and of restaurants after the price rise.
PTS:
- Petrol: demand is inelastic, so the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the price rise and total revenue rises (1)
- Restaurants: demand is elastic, so the fall in quantity outweighs the price rise and total revenue falls (1)
PART (c) 2: Explain why the government might prefer to tax petrol rather than restaurant meals if its aim is to raise revenue.
PTS:
- Because demand for petrol is inelastic, a tax barely reduces the quantity bought (1)
- So the tax base stays large and the tax raises a lot of revenue with little change in behaviour (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P2i-tax
c: P2i.3 P5b.5 P2i.2
lv: 3
k: calc explain
LEAD: Before a tax, 10,000 bottles of a sugary drink are sold each week at $3.00 a bottle. The government places a tax of $0.50 on each bottle. Afterwards, 9,500 bottles a week are sold at $3.40.
PART (a) 1: Calculate the government's weekly revenue from the tax.
ANS: $0.50 × 9,500 = $4,750 a week (the tax per unit times the quantity sold after the tax).
PART (b) 2: Calculate how much of the $0.50 tax is paid by consumers and how much by producers.
ANS: Consumers pay $0.40 more ($3.40 − $3.00). Producers now keep $2.90 a bottle ($3.40 − $0.50), $0.10 less than before. So consumers bear $0.40 and producers $0.10 of each $0.50.
PART (c) 2: Explain why consumers bear most of this tax.
PTS:
- Demand is price inelastic: the price rose by about 13% but the quantity bought fell by only 5% (PED about 0.4), so buyers kept buying at the higher price (1)
- So producers could pass most of the tax on in a higher price: the economic incidence falls mainly on consumers, even though producers pay the tax to the government (legal incidence) (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P3a-mpc
c: P3a.4 P3a.5
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows a household's weekly disposable income and consumption.
TABLE: Income and consumption ($ a week)
| Disposable income | Consumption |
| 800 | 760 |
| 1,000 | 920 |
| 1,200 | 1,080 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the marginal propensity to consume when income rises from $1,000 to $1,200.
ANS: MPC = change in consumption ÷ change in income = (1,080 − 920) ÷ (1,200 − 1,000) = 160 ÷ 200 = 0.8.
PART (b) 1: Calculate the marginal propensity to save.
ANS: MPS = 1 − MPC = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2 (the household saves 20 cents of each extra dollar).
PART (c) 2: Explain what happens to the household's saving as its income rises.
PTS:
- Saving (income − consumption) rises from $40 to $80 to $120 a week (1)
- The share of income saved also rises, from 5% to about 8% to 10%: as income rises, households can save a larger proportion (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P3c-profit
c: P3c.2
lv: 2
k: calc explain
LEAD: Mia runs a bakery. Last year it earned revenue of $420,000 and paid $250,000 for ingredients, rent and wages. Mia put $60,000 of her own savings into the business, which could have earned 5 per cent interest in a bank, and she gave up a job paying $85,000 a year.
PART (a) 1: Calculate the bakery's accounting profit.
ANS: Accounting profit = revenue − explicit costs = 420,000 − 250,000 = $170,000.
PART (b) 2: Calculate the bakery's economic profit.
ANS: Implicit (opportunity) costs = 85,000 + (5% × 60,000) = 85,000 + 3,000 = $88,000. Economic profit = 170,000 − 88,000 = $82,000.
PART (c) 2: Explain why economic profit is a better guide than accounting profit to whether Mia should keep running the bakery.
PTS:
- Economic profit also counts opportunity costs: the income from her next best job and the interest on her savings (1)
- A positive economic profit ($82,000) shows the bakery earns more than her resources would earn elsewhere, so staying in business is worthwhile (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P3c-prod
c: P3c.4 P3c.5
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows output and hours worked at a factory before and after it installed new machinery.
TABLE: Output and hours worked
| | Before | After |
| Output (units a week) | 12,000 | 15,000 |
| Hours worked a week | 2,400 | 2,500 |
PART (a) 2: Calculate labour productivity (output per hour worked) before and after, and the percentage change.
ANS: Before: 12,000 ÷ 2,400 = 5 units an hour. After: 15,000 ÷ 2,500 = 6 units an hour. Change = (6 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = 20 per cent.
PART (b) 2: Explain how the new machinery raised productivity.
PTS:
- It is capital deepening: each worker has more and better capital to work with (1)
- So more output is produced in each hour of work, lowering the cost of each unit (1)
PART (c) 2: Explain why higher productivity is important for living standards.
PTS:
- Higher output per hour allows higher real wages without raising unit costs or prices (1)
- It raises the economy's productive capacity, so incomes and living standards can rise over time (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P4c-mortgage
c: P4c.7 P4c.3
lv: 2
k: calc explain
LEAD: A household has a home loan of $600,000. Ignoring repayments of the amount borrowed, its yearly interest cost is the loan multiplied by the interest rate.
PART (a) 1: Calculate the yearly interest at interest rates of 5 per cent and 6 per cent.
ANS: At 5%: $600,000 × 0.05 = $30,000. At 6%: $600,000 × 0.06 = $36,000, which is $6,000 a year (about $500 a month) more.
PART (b) 3: Explain how a rise in interest rates from 5 to 6 per cent affects housing affordability.
PTS:
- Existing borrowers pay more interest, leaving less income for other spending (1)
- New buyers can borrow less for the same repayment, because banks test whether they can afford repayments (1)
- Fewer people can afford to buy, so demand for housing falls, which may slow or reduce house prices over time (1)

=== sa y11-sd-P5b-tax
c: P5b.6 P5b.4
lv: 2
k: calc explain
LEAD: In a hypothetical income tax system, no tax is paid on the first $20,000 of income, 20 per cent is paid on income from $20,001 to $60,000, and 40 per cent on income above $60,000.
PART (a) 2: Calculate the tax paid, and the average tax rate, for a person earning $80,000.
ANS: 20% × 40,000 = $8,000 plus 40% × 20,000 = $8,000, so the tax is $16,000. The average tax rate is 16,000 ÷ 80,000 = 20 per cent.
PART (b) 1: Identify this person's marginal tax rate.
ANS: 40 per cent, the rate on each extra dollar of income above $60,000.
PART (c) 2: Using a person earning $40,000 for comparison, explain why this tax is progressive.
PTS:
- At $40,000 the tax is 20% × 20,000 = $4,000, an average rate of 10 per cent (1)
- The average tax rate rises with income (10 per cent at $40,000, 20 per cent at $80,000), so higher earners pay a larger share of their income (1)
