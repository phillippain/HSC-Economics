Q: Explain how the multiplier affects the impact of a change in government spending on national income.
PTS:
- A rise in government spending is an injection that becomes income for others (1)
- Recipients spend part of each extra dollar (the MPC), creating further rounds of income (1)
- National income rises by a multiple of the original change: ΔY = ΔG × k, where k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) (1)

Q: Distinguish between cyclical and structural unemployment, using an example of each.
PTS:
- Cyclical: caused by a lack of aggregate demand in a downturn (1)
- Example, e.g. retail or construction jobs lost in a recession (1)
- Structural: caused by a mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, often from technology or changes in industry (1)
- Example, e.g. car manufacturing workers after the closure of car plants in 2017 (1)

Q: Explain the relationship between the NAIRU and inflation.
PTS:
- The NAIRU is the lowest unemployment rate that can be sustained without inflation rising (1)
- Below the NAIRU, tight labour markets push wage growth up, raising costs and inflation (1)
- Above it, spare capacity keeps wage growth and inflation subdued; structural reform can lower the NAIRU (1)

Q: Explain why the RBA pays close attention to underlying inflation as well as headline inflation.
PTS:
- Headline inflation can be swung by volatile or one-off price changes such as fuel, fruit or government rebates (1)
- Underlying measures such as the trimmed mean remove extreme price changes, showing the trend in inflation (1)
- Monetary policy works with lags, so the RBA responds to the persistent trend rather than temporary changes (1)

Q: Discuss the effects of a continued current account deficit on the Australian economy.
PTS:
- A CAD allows investment higher than national saving, funding growth (1)
- It adds to foreign liabilities, so more income is paid overseas (net primary income), widening the CAD further (1)
- A high level of foreign debt can raise the risk premium on borrowing and make the economy vulnerable to a loss of investor confidence (1)
- It can lead to downward pressure on the exchange rate and constrain policy (1)
- Balanced judgement, e.g. sustainable if borrowing funds productive investment (the Pitchford view) (1)

Q: Explain how automatic stabilisers in the budget help reduce fluctuations in economic activity.
PTS:
- Automatic stabilisers are parts of the budget that change with the cycle without new decisions (1)
- In a downturn, tax revenue falls and welfare payments rise, supporting incomes and AD (1)
- In a boom, tax revenue rises and welfare payments fall, restraining AD (1)
- So the budget moves towards deficit in downturns and surplus in booms, smoothing the cycle (1)

Q: Outline how the RBA implements a change in monetary policy.
PTS:
- The Monetary Policy Board decides the target for the cash rate and announces it (1)
- The RBA manages the supply of money in the overnight market (exchange settlement balances), through open market operations and the interest rates on its standing facilities, so the cash rate settles at the target (1)
- Banks pass the change through to lending and deposit rates (1)

PTS:

Q: Compare the use of regulation and market-based policies to reduce pollution.
PTS:
- Regulation sets rules, such as emission standards or bans, enforced by penalties (1)
- Market-based policies, such as taxes or emissions trading, put a price on pollution (1)

Q: Explain how time lags limit the effectiveness of fiscal policy.
PTS:
- Recognition lag: data are delayed, so problems are identified late (1)
- Implementation lag: budget changes need planning and laws passed by Parliament (1)
- Impact lag: spending and tax changes take time to affect demand, so policy may take effect when it is no longer needed (1)
