# Data questions in the style of HSC Section II: a table, then parts that move from calculating to explaining.

=== sa sd-3B1-lf
c: 3B.1 3B.2
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
ss: S3.12
like: II2014Q23(a) II2014Q23(b)
LEAD: The table shows labour market data for a hypothetical economy.
TABLE: Labour market data ('000)
| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| Employed | 14,000 | 14,200 |
| Unemployed | 600 | 760 |
| Working-age population | 22,000 | 22,400 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the unemployment rate in Year 2.
ANS: Labour force = 14,200 + 760 = 14,960 thousand. Unemployment rate = 760 ÷ 14,960 × 100 = 5.1%.
PART (b) 1: Calculate the participation rate in Year 2.
ANS: 14,960 ÷ 22,400 × 100 = 66.8%.
PART (c) 2: Explain why the unemployment rate rose even though employment increased.
PTS:
- The labour force grew faster than employment: it rose by 360,000 (2.5%) while employment rose by 200,000 (1.4%) (1)
- More people entered the labour force looking for work (participation rose from 66.4% to 66.8%), so the number unemployed rose (1)
PART (d) 3: Explain ONE policy the government could use to reduce structural unemployment.
PTS:
- Identifies a suitable policy, e.g. education and training or apprenticeship programs (1)
- Explains how it addresses the skills mismatch between workers and the jobs available (1)
- Explains the effect, e.g. more employable workers, lower long-term unemployment, possibly a lower NAIRU (1)

=== sa sd-2B1-bop
c: 2B.1 2B.2 2B.3
lv: 2
k: calc table analyse
ss: S2.6 S2.7
like: II2023Q22(a) II2011Q22(a)(i)
LEAD: The table shows balance of payments data for a hypothetical economy. Net errors and omissions are zero.
TABLE: Balance of payments ($ billion)
| Item | $ billion |
| Exports of goods | 480 |
| Imports of goods | 450 |
| Exports of services | 110 |
| Imports of services | 125 |
| Net primary income | −75 |
| Net secondary income | −2 |
| Balance on capital account | −1 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the balance on goods and services.
ANS: (480 − 450) + (110 − 125) = 30 − 15 = $15 billion (a surplus).
PART (b) 1: Calculate the balance on current account.
ANS: 15 + (−75) + (−2) = −$62 billion (a deficit).
PART (c) 1: Calculate the balance on the financial account.
ANS: Current account + capital account + financial account = 0, so −62 + (−1) + financial account = 0: the financial account is +$63 billion (a surplus).
PART (d) 3: Explain the relationship between the financial account surplus and the net primary income deficit.
PTS:
- The financial account surplus shows foreign borrowing and investment flowing in to fund the current account deficit (1)
- These inflows add to the economy's foreign debt and equity liabilities (1)
- Servicing them (interest and dividends) is recorded as debits in net primary income, so the deficit grows over time and must itself be financed (1)

=== sa sd-2C1-xr
c: 2C.1 2C.3 2C.6
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows the value of the Australian dollar in two years (hypothetical data).
TABLE: Exchange rates (foreign currency per A$1) and the Trade Weighted Index
| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| US dollar | 0.68 | 0.64 |
| Japanese yen | 98.0 | 104.0 |
| Euro | 0.60 | 0.58 |
| TWI (May 1970 = 100) | 63.0 | 62.4 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the percentage change in the value of the A$ against the US dollar.
ANS: (0.64 − 0.68) ÷ 0.68 × 100 = −5.9%, a depreciation.
PART (b) 1: Identify the currency in the table against which the A$ appreciated.
ANS: The Japanese yen (one A$ buys 104 yen instead of 98).
PART (c) 2: Explain why the TWI fell by much less than the A$ fell against the US dollar.
PTS:
- The TWI measures the A$ against a basket of trading partners' currencies, weighted by trade (1)
- The A$ rose against some currencies (the yen) and fell less against others, so the fall against the US dollar was partly offset (1)
PART (d) 2: Explain how this change in the exchange rate could affect Australian exporters.
PTS:
- Australian exports become cheaper in US dollar terms, so exporters can sell more (1)
- Contracts priced in US dollars earn more A$, lifting exporters' revenue and profits (1)

=== sa sd-4C1-budget
c: 4C.1 4C.2 4C.3
lv: 3
k: calc table explain
LEAD: The table shows budget data for a hypothetical economy.
TABLE: Government budget ($ billion)
| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| Revenue | 680 | 690 |
| Expenditure | 700 | 735 |
| GDP | 2,700 | 2,780 |
PART (a) 2: Calculate the budget outcome in Year 2, in dollars and as a percentage of GDP.
ANS: 690 − 735 = −$45 billion, a deficit of 45 ÷ 2,780 × 100 = 1.6% of GDP.
PART (b) 1: Identify the change in the budget stance from Year 1 to Year 2.
ANS: More expansionary: the deficit widened from $20 billion (0.7% of GDP) to $45 billion (1.6% of GDP).
PART (c) 2: Explain ONE reason the deficit could have widened without any new policy decision.
PTS:
- Automatic stabilisers: a slowdown lowers tax revenue and raises welfare payments (1)
- So the cyclical component of the budget moves towards deficit, even though policy settings are unchanged (1)
PART (d) 3: Explain how the government could finance this deficit and ONE possible effect.
PTS:
- By selling Commonwealth Government Securities (bonds) to investors in Australia or overseas (1)
- Effect explained, e.g. borrowing competes for funds and may raise interest rates (crowding out) (1)
- Or: borrowing from overseas adds to foreign debt and future interest payments in the current account (1)

=== sa sd-3A2-flow
c: 3A.2 3A.3
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
ss: S3.9 S3.10 S3.11
like: II2016Q24(a) II2013Q22(a)
LEAD: The table shows data for a hypothetical economy. The marginal propensity to consume is 0.75.
TABLE: Leakages and injections ($ billion)
| Item | $ billion |
| Savings | 120 |
| Taxation | 200 |
| Imports | 140 |
| Investment | 130 |
| Government spending | 210 |
| Exports | 110 |
PART (a) 1: State whether national income will rise, fall or stay the same. Give a reason.
ANS: It will fall: withdrawals (120 + 200 + 140 = $460b) exceed injections (130 + 210 + 110 = $450b).
PART (b) 1: Calculate the value of the multiplier.
ANS: k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4.
PART (c) 1: Calculate the change in national income if government spending rises by $10 billion.
ANS: $10 billion × 4 = $40 billion increase.
PART (d) 2: Explain the implications of the multiplier for fluctuations in economic activity.
PTS:
- Small changes in spending (such as investment or exports) cause larger changes in national income (1)
- So shocks are magnified into larger booms and downturns; a larger multiplier means larger fluctuations (1)

=== sa sd-3E1-quint
c: 3E.1 3E.3
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
ss: S3.13
LEAD: The table shows the share of income received by each fifth (quintile) of households in a hypothetical economy.
TABLE: Share of income by quintile (%)
| Quintile | Market income | Disposable income |
| Lowest 20% | 2 | 7 |
| Second | 6 | 12 |
| Third | 15 | 17 |
| Fourth | 25 | 23 |
| Highest 20% | 52 | 41 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the ratio of the highest quintile's share of disposable income to the lowest quintile's share.
ANS: 41 ÷ 7 = 5.9 to 1.
PART (b) 2: Identify which distribution is more equal, and explain why.
PTS:
- Disposable income is more equal: the top quintile's share falls from 52% to 41% and the bottom's rises from 2% to 7% (1)
- Because progressive income tax takes more from high incomes and transfer payments add to low incomes (1)
PART (c) 3: Explain how a Lorenz curve for disposable income would differ from one for market income.
PTS:
- The Lorenz curve for disposable income lies closer to the line of equality (1)
- Area A (between the line of equality and the curve) is smaller (1)
- So the Gini coefficient for disposable income is lower than for market income (1)

=== sa sd-1C2-tariff
c: 1C.2
lv: 2
k: calc table explain
like: II2024Q21(a)(i) II2024Q21(a)(ii)
LEAD: The table shows the domestic market for a good. The world price is $40, and the government imposes a tariff of $10 a unit.
TABLE: Domestic demand and supply ('000 units)
| Price ($) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied by domestic firms |
| 40 | 120 | 30 |
| 50 | 110 | 45 |
PART (a) 1: Calculate the quantity imported before the tariff.
ANS: 120 − 30 = 90,000 units.
PART (b) 1: Calculate the quantity imported after the tariff.
ANS: 110 − 45 = 65,000 units.
PART (c) 1: Calculate the government's revenue from the tariff.
ANS: $10 × 65,000 = $650,000.
PART (d) 3: Explain the effect of the tariff on the allocation of resources.
PTS:
- Domestic production rises from 30,000 to 45,000 units, drawing resources into a less efficient industry (1)
- Consumers buy less and pay more, so resources are not allocated to their most valued uses (1)
- The result is a deadweight loss: production and consumption losses that no one gains (1)

=== sa sd-1D3-hdi
c: 1D.3 1D.4 1D.1
lv: 2
k: table explain
LEAD: The table shows development indicators for three countries.
TABLE: Development indicators
| | Country A | Country B | Country C |
| GNI per person (PPP, US$) | 62,000 | 18,000 | 2,200 |
| Life expectancy (years) | 83 | 77 | 62 |
| Mean years of schooling | 12.7 | 8.0 | 4.5 |
PART (a) 1: Identify which country is most likely a developing economy.
ANS: Country C: the lowest income, life expectancy and schooling.
PART (b) 3: Explain why GNI per person alone is a limited measure of a country's level of development.
PTS:
- It measures average income, not how it is distributed (1)
- It leaves out health, education, freedom and the environment, which are part of wellbeing (1)
- Two countries with similar incomes can have very different life expectancy and schooling (1)
PART (c) 2: Outline how the Human Development Index gives a fuller picture.
PTS:
- It combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life, knowledge and a decent standard of living (1)
- Each is measured (life expectancy, years of schooling, GNI per person at PPP) and combined in an index from 0 to 1 (1)
