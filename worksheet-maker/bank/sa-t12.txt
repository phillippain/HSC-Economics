Q: Explain how changes in technology have increased world trade in goods and services.
PTS:
- Containerisation and larger ships have cut the cost of moving goods (1)
- The internet and digital platforms let services such as education, finance and design be traded across borders (1)

Q: Explain how a downturn in the international business cycle can affect the Australian economy.
PTS:
- Weaker growth overseas lowers demand for Australian exports, especially resources (1)
- Lower export volumes and prices reduce the terms of trade, national income and investment in the resources sector (1)
- Growth slows and unemployment rises in Australia; the dollar may depreciate, which partly cushions the effect (1)

Q: Explain how free trade can increase living standards in both countries that trade.
PTS:
- Each country specialises in goods in which it has a comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost) (1)
- Specialisation raises total output, so world production rises (1)
- Through trade, each country consumes beyond its own production possibilities (1)
- Consumers benefit from lower prices and more choice; firms gain economies of scale from larger markets (1)

Q: Assess the advantages and disadvantages of bilateral free trade agreements compared with multilateral agreements.
PTS:
- Advantage: bilateral agreements are quicker and easier to negotiate than multilateral deals that need every WTO member to agree (1)
- Advantage: they can go further, covering services, investment and other areas (1)
- Disadvantage: they can cause trade diversion away from lower-cost non-members (1)
- Disadvantage: many overlapping agreements with different rules of origin (a "noodle bowl") raise compliance costs; judgement on balance (1)

Q: Explain the infant industry argument for protection.
PTS:
- A new industry has higher average costs than established overseas firms because it has not yet reached a large scale (1)
- Temporary protection lets it grow and gain economies of scale and experience, lowering costs (1)
- Once it can compete, protection should be removed; the risk is that the industry never becomes competitive and protection becomes permanent (1)

PTS:
- Economic growth is an increase in real GDP, the volume of goods and services produced (1)

Q: Explain why the global distribution of income has changed since 1990.
PTS:
- Rapid growth in large emerging economies, especially China and India, has raised incomes for billions of people (1)
- This has narrowed the gap between countries and greatly reduced extreme poverty (1)
- Inequality within many countries has risen, as gains from globalisation and technology went mostly to owners of capital and skilled workers (1)
- Many developing economies, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, have grown more slowly, so some gaps remain wide (1)

=== sa sa-2B1-01
c: 2B.1 2B.2
m: 4
lv: 2
k: explain
Q: Explain how the purchase of an Australian business by a foreign company affects Australia's balance of payments, now and in the future.
PTS:
- Now: the purchase is a capital inflow, recorded as a credit (foreign direct investment in Australia) in the financial account (1)
- It adds to Australia's foreign liabilities (net foreign equity) (1)
- In future: profits and dividends paid to the foreign owners are debits in net primary income (1)
- This widens the current account deficit over time (1)

=== sa sa-2B3-01
c: 2B.3 2A.2
m: 4
lv: 3
k: analyse
Q: Analyse the relationship between the balance on the capital and financial account and the net primary income balance.
PTS:
- A surplus on the capital and financial account means foreign borrowing and investment flow into Australia (1)
- These inflows add to Australia's foreign debt and foreign equity liabilities (1)
- Servicing the liabilities requires interest and dividend payments overseas, debits in net primary income (1)
- So persistent financial account surpluses lead to larger net primary income deficits, which widen the current account deficit and must themselves be financed: a cycle (1)

Q: Explain how a rise in the terms of trade affects Australia's balance of payments.
PTS:
- The terms of trade rise when export prices rise relative to import prices (1)
- Export income rises, so the balance on goods and services improves (1)
- The current account deficit narrows (or a surplus appears, as in 2019–20 to 2022–23) (1)
- Profits of foreign-owned mining firms rise, so more dividends flow overseas, widening the net primary income deficit and partly offsetting the gain (1)

Q: Compare a floating exchange rate with a fixed exchange rate.
PTS:
- Floating: the rate is set by supply and demand; the central bank intervenes rarely (1)
- Fixed: the government or central bank sets the rate and defends it by buying or selling currency (1)
- Floating: monetary policy can target inflation, and the rate absorbs shocks such as a fall in commodity prices (1)
- Fixed: gives certainty to traders, but needs large reserves and can be attacked by speculators (1)

Q: Analyse the effects of a sustained depreciation of the Australian dollar on the Australian economy.
PTS:
- Exports become cheaper overseas and imports dearer, raising international competitiveness (1)
- Net exports and aggregate demand rise, lifting growth and employment (1)
- Import prices rise, adding to inflation (imported inflation) (1)
- The trade balance may worsen first and then improve (J-curve); the A$ cost of foreign-currency debt rises (1)
- Valuation effects: foreign-currency assets are worth more in A$, which can lower net foreign liabilities (1)

Q: Explain the effects of tariff reductions on Australian firms and consumers.
PTS:
- Consumers pay lower prices for imported goods and have more choice, raising real incomes (1)
- Firms that use imported inputs gain lower costs, improving competitiveness (1)
- Import-competing firms face more competition; some may close, causing structural unemployment (1)
- Resources move to more efficient, export-oriented industries in the long run (1)

Q: Explain how protectionist policies of other countries affect Australia.
PTS:
- Tariffs or quotas on Australian goods reduce export volumes and prices, e.g. China's duties on Australian wine and barley from 2020 (1)
- Export income and employment in the affected industries fall (1)
- Subsidies overseas (e.g. EU and US farm support) lower world prices, hurting Australian exporters in third markets (1)
- Trade wars between major economies slow world growth and divert goods to Australia, affecting Australian firms indirectly (1)
