What export incentives are

Export incentives are government measures that help local firms sell overseas. They range from export subsidies, which pay producers for each unit they export, to non-price assistance such as marketing grants, trade missions, information about foreign markets and help with finance and insurance. They protect local industry indirectly, by giving it a larger market than it could win on its own.

How an export subsidy works

When the world price Pw is above the local equilibrium, a country exports the gap between what its firms supply and what its consumers buy (Q2 to Q3 in Figure 2). An export subsidy of s per unit means producers earn Pw+s on every unit they export, so they will not sell locally for less. The local price rises, local consumers buy less (Q1), producers supply more (Q4), and exports grow to Q1–Q4. Taxpayers pay the subsidy on every exported unit (the gold rectangle).

If the exporting country is large, the extra supply pushes the world price down. That hurts efficient, unsubsidised exporters in other countries. This is why Australian farmers have long opposed the export subsidies of the EU and the US.

Export incentives in Australia

  • The Export Market Development Grants (EMDG) scheme reimburses part of small firms' overseas marketing costs. It has about $105 million in 2026–27 and has helped more than 51,000 exporters since 1974.
  • Austrade gives market information and trade missions, and Export Finance Australia, a government agency, provides loans, guarantees and insurance when banks will not.
  • These are non-price incentives: they do not cut the price of exports, so they are allowed under WTO rules and distort trade far less than export subsidies.

Export subsidies in the world

At the WTO's Nairobi meeting in 2015, members agreed to abolish export subsidies on farm goods, a long-standing aim of the Cairns Group led by Australia. But farm support continues through other channels: the EU's Common Agricultural Policy still takes more than a third of the EU budget, and ABARES estimates that rich-country farm support costs Australia $8 to $10 billion a year in lost farm exports.

Evaluating export incentives

  • For: they help small firms overcome the cost of entering new markets, lift export income and jobs, and can help diversify exports away from one or two buyers.
  • Against: export subsidies cost taxpayers, raise local prices, misallocate resources and lower world prices for other exporters, and they can provoke countervailing duties.

Use it in an exam: distinguish export subsidies (price support, WTO-banned for farm goods) from non-price assistance such as the EMDG. Explain how foreign export subsidies harm Australia through lower world prices for its farm exports.
