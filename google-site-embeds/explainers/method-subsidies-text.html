What a subsidy is

A subsidy (or bounty) is a payment or tax concession from the government to local producers. It lowers their costs, so they can sell more at the world price and compete with imports. Subsidies can be cash grants, tax credits, cheap loans or guaranteed purchases.

How a subsidy works

Because the subsidy cuts costs, local firms are willing to supply more at every price: the supply curve shifts right from S1 to S2 (Figure 2). Imports are still available at the world price, so the price consumers pay does not change.

  • Price: stays at Pw, so consumers are not directly affected.
  • Local production: rises from Q1 to Q2, protecting local firms and jobs.
  • Consumption: stays at Q3.
  • Imports: fall from Q1–Q3 to Q2–Q3.
  • Cost: taxpayers pay the subsidy on every unit produced (the subsidy per unit times Q2). The money has an opportunity cost: it could have funded schools or hospitals, or lower taxes.

Subsidy or tariff?

A subsidy protects local producers without raising prices to consumers, so it causes less deadweight loss than a tariff: there is no loss from reduced consumption, only from inefficient production. It is also transparent, because its cost appears in the Budget. But it must be paid for, and it is hard to remove once firms depend on it. Other countries may also treat subsidised exports as unfair and impose countervailing duties.

Subsidies now

  • Australia: the Whyalla steelworks received $2.4 billion in support plus $223 million more. Under Future Made in Australia, production tax incentives pay 10 per cent of the cost of processing critical minerals and $2 for each kilogram of green hydrogen.
  • Farm support: government support is under 3 per cent of farm income in Australia, against about 7 per cent in the US, 16 per cent in the EU and 41 per cent in South Korea. The EU's Common Agricultural Policy takes more than a third of the EU budget.
  • Industry policy: the US, the EU and China all subsidise electric vehicles, batteries, chips and clean energy, which has started subsidy races and trade disputes over “overcapacity”.

Use it in an exam: show the supply curve shifting right at an unchanged world price, then compare with a tariff: same protection for local producers, no rise in consumer prices, but a direct cost to the Budget.
