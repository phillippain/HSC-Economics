What a tariff is

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. It is the most common method of protection. An ad valorem tariff is a percentage of the import's value (for example 5 per cent); a specific tariff is a fixed amount per unit (for example $2 a kilogram). The tariff is collected at the border, so imports cost more than locally made goods.

How a tariff works

In a small economy like Australia, imports are available at the world price (Pw). Local firms supply Q1, consumers buy Q4, and imports fill the gap (Figure 2). A tariff raises the price of imports to Pw+t, and local producers can then charge the same higher price. The effects are often remembered as five:

  • Price effect: the domestic price rises from Pw to Pw+t.
  • Production (protection) effect: local firms expand output from Q1 to Q2.
  • Consumption effect: consumers buy less, Q3 instead of Q4.
  • Revenue effect: the government collects the tariff on the imports that remain (Q2 to Q3), the gold rectangle.
  • Redistribution effect: income moves from consumers to local producers and the government. The two red triangles are deadweight losses: inefficient local production and consumption that no longer happens.

Worked example

Suppose S is Q = P and D is Q = 100 − P, with a world price of $20. Local output is 20 and demand 80, so imports are 60. A $15 tariff lifts the price to $35: local output rises to 35, demand falls to 65 and imports fall to 30. Tariff revenue is $15 × 30 = $450, and the deadweight loss is two triangles of $112.50, or $225 in total (Figure 3).

Tariffs in Australia and the world

Australia's average tariff fell from about 36 per cent in 1968–69 to about 0.4 per cent in 2025. About 90 per cent of imports now enter tariff-free, the general tariff is 5 per cent or less, and tariff revenue is about $1.9 billion, only 0.2 per cent of Commonwealth revenue. The government removed about 500 “nuisance” tariffs in 2024 and more than 1,000 since 2022. The world has gone the other way: the US placed a 12.5 per cent tariff on Australian goods from July 2026, and the WTO counted a record share of world imports under restrictions in 2026.

Use it in an exam: draw the diagram, label Pw, Pw+t and Q1 to Q4, and walk through the five effects. Then judge: who gains (local producers, government), who loses (consumers, firms using imported inputs, exporters if other countries retaliate), and the net loss to the economy.
