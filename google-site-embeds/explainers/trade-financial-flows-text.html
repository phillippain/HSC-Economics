What trade and financial flows are

Australia is linked to the global economy in two main ways. Trade flows are exports and imports of goods and services. Financial flows are movements of money for investment: foreigners buying Australian assets or lending to Australians, and Australians doing the same overseas. The syllabus asks about three features of each flow:

  • Value: how large the flow is, in dollars or as a share of gross domestic product (GDP).
  • Composition: what the flow is made of, such as minerals, services, debt or equity.
  • Direction: which countries the flow goes to and comes from.

The value of Australia's trade

Australia is a small open economy that depends heavily on trade. In 2025 it exported $665.2 billion of goods and services and imported $658.3 billion, a trade surplus of $6.9 billion. Exports are worth close to a quarter of GDP. Exports grew 3.1 per cent in 2025 but imports grew 6.9 per cent, and by the June quarter of 2026 the balance on goods and services had moved into a deficit of $5.1 billion.

The value of exports swings with world commodity prices. The terms of trade compare the prices Australia receives for exports with the prices it pays for imports:

Terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index × 100

A rise in the terms of trade means the same volume of exports pays for more imports, so national income rises. Figure 1 shows the latest change.

The composition of trade

Australia's exports reflect its comparative advantage in resources. Four commodities (iron ore, coal, natural gas and gold) made up 45.8 per cent of all exports in 2025 (Figure 2). Services earned $142.1 billion, or 21.4 per cent of exports. Education for international students was the fifth largest export at $55.0 billion. Rural goods such as beef and wheat remain important, but manufactured goods are a small share.

Two long-term trends stand out. First, exports have shifted from farm products to minerals and energy, driven by the industrialisation of Asia. Second, services have grown as incomes in the region have risen. Narrow exports carry a risk: when coal prices fell in 2025, the value of coal exports dropped 24.7 per cent.

Imports are far more varied. The largest items in 2025 were overseas travel by Australians ($68.5 billion), refined petroleum ($43.9 billion) and passenger motor vehicles ($34.5 billion). Australia mostly imports manufactured consumer goods, capital goods such as machinery and computers, and fuel. Australia runs a surplus on goods and a deficit on services, which was $37.5 billion in 2025.

The direction of trade

In the 1950s the United Kingdom was Australia's largest market. Japan took over from the late 1960s, and China has been the largest trading partner since the late 2000s. Today most of Australia's trade is with Asia.

China bought 29.4 per cent of Australia's exports in 2025, three times the share of Japan in second place. The top five markets took 59.8 per cent of exports (Figure 3). China is also the largest source of imports with 19.8 per cent, followed by the United States with 15.0 per cent.

This concentration brings benefits and risks. Strong demand from China lifts commodity prices, export income and the terms of trade (Figure 4). The risk is that a slowdown in China, or a trade dispute, hits Australia harder than a more diversified economy (Figure 5). Free trade agreements with other partners are one way the government has tried to spread this risk.

Financial flows: debt and equity

Financial flows have grown faster than trade since the Australian dollar was floated in 1983 and controls on capital were removed. They take two forms (Figure 6).

  • Debt: borrowing from overseas. Australian banks, firms and governments sell bonds to foreign lenders or take foreign loans. The lender receives interest and must be repaid.
  • Equity: ownership. Foreigners buy shares, property or whole businesses in Australia. They receive dividends or profits, and no repayment is required.

Investment is also classified by the degree of control. Direct investment is a stake of 10 per cent or more, which gives the investor a say in running the business. Portfolio investment is a smaller holding of shares or bonds, bought for the financial return. Portfolio flows can leave quickly, so they are more volatile.

Trends in financial flows

Australia has long been a net importer of capital. Its national saving is too low to pay for all its investment, so the gap is filled from overseas. This inflow appears as a surplus on the capital and financial account, which offsets the current account deficit.

At the end of 2025 foreigners had $5,116.3 billion invested in Australia, and Australians had $4,477.8 billion invested abroad (Figure 7). The United States (26.6 per cent) and the United Kingdom (16.4 per cent) are the largest investors. China is the largest trading partner but holds only 1.7 per cent of foreign investment here. The direction of financial flows is very different from the direction of trade.

The composition has changed too. Compulsory superannuation has built a large pool of savings, and super funds have bought shares overseas. Australians now own more foreign equity than foreigners own Australian equity, a net equity asset of $847.1 billion in June 2026. Australia still owes much more debt than it is owed: net foreign debt was $1,486.0 billion. Together these give net foreign liabilities of $638.9 billion.

Effects on Australia's economic performance

  • Economic growth: exports add to aggregate demand, and foreign investment pays for mines, factories and infrastructure that domestic saving could not.
  • Exposure to the world: a fall in world growth or commodity prices quickly cuts export income, company profits and tax revenue.
  • Income outflows: past borrowing and foreign ownership must be serviced. Interest and dividends paid overseas are the main cause of the current account deficit.
  • The exchange rate: both flows change the demand for and supply of Australian dollars.
  • Structural change: resources move toward industries with a comparative advantage, such as mining and education, and away from import-competing manufacturing.

Links to other parts of the course

  • Balance of payments: trade flows are recorded on the current account, and financial flows on the capital and financial account.
  • Exchange rates: higher commodity prices and capital inflow both tend to appreciate the dollar.
  • External stability: net foreign debt and net foreign liabilities are the stocks built up by past financial flows.

Use it in an exam: cover value, composition and direction for both trade and financial flows. Quote the share of exports going to China, name the top four commodity exports, and separate debt from equity. Then explain the effect on growth, the current account and the exchange rate.
