What external stability means

External stability is the ability of the economy to pay its way in the world over time. It is achieved when the current account deficit (CAD) and the foreign liabilities that build up from it are at levels that foreign lenders and investors regard as sustainable. If they lose confidence, they may stop lending or demand higher interest rates, and the exchange rate and living standards are put under pressure.

External stability does not require a current account balance of zero. Australia has run current account deficits in most years since the 1960s because it invests more than it saves, and the gap is filled by foreign savings. What matters is whether the deficit and the debt it creates are manageable relative to the size of the economy.

How external stability is measured

The syllabus names six measures (Figure 3). The first three are ratios to gross domestic product (GDP), which allows for the size of the economy.

  • CAD as a percentage of GDP = current account deficit ÷ GDP × 100. A rising ratio means Australia is borrowing more from the world relative to its income.
  • Net foreign debt as a percentage of GDP: the borrowing from overseas (loans and bonds) minus Australian lending abroad, divided by GDP. It must be repaid with interest.
  • Net foreign liabilities as a percentage of GDP: net foreign debt plus net foreign equity. Equity is foreign ownership of Australian shares and businesses less Australian ownership of foreign assets. It is Australia's total net obligation to the rest of the world.
  • Terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index × 100. A rise increases export income and improves the CAD.
  • Exchange rate: a sharp depreciation raises the cost of servicing foreign currency debt and signals low confidence. A moderate fall helps competitiveness.
  • International competitiveness: unit labour costs, productivity and quality compared with other countries. Better competitiveness raises exports and cuts imports.

Illustration: if the CAD is $60 billion and GDP is $2,400 billion, the CAD is 60 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 2.5 per cent of GDP.

Trends

The CAD has moved between surplus and deficit with the commodity cycle. The surpluses of 2020 to 2022 came from high export prices for iron ore, coal and gas. Since then the terms of trade have fallen from their 2022 peak and the deficit has returned (Figure 2).

In the June quarter 2026 the current account deficit was $27.2 billion, the largest of the past five quarters and expected to be the highest share of GDP since mid-2016. The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) said record imports of fuel and electric vehicles pushed the balance on goods and services into a second quarterly deficit (Figure 4).

The main driver is the net primary income deficit of $21.9 billion in the quarter. This is the interest, dividends and profits paid to foreign owners and lenders, less what Australians earn overseas. It is a cost of past borrowing and foreign investment, and it makes up most of the deficit.

At 30 June 2026 Australia's net international investment liability was $638.9 billion. Net foreign debt was $1,486.0 billion, partly offset by a net foreign equity asset position of $847.1 billion (Figure 5). Australians own more foreign shares than foreigners own of Australia, but the debt is much larger. The fall of $122.8 billion in the quarter came from higher overseas share prices, not from a smaller deficit.

Causes

  • Savings–investment gap: national saving is lower than investment, so the shortfall is borrowed from overseas (Figure 6).
  • Low national saving: private household saving and government budget deficits both reduce the pool of domestic savings.
  • Commodity dependence: export income swings with world prices, so the terms of trade and the CAD move with the cycle.
  • Strong domestic demand: when growth is faster than that of trading partners, imports rise.
  • Weak international competitiveness: higher costs than rivals reduce exports of manufactures and services.
  • Foreign investment: the income paid to foreign owners adds to the primary income deficit.

Effects

Positive effects

  • Foreign savings fund investment above what Australia can fund itself, such as mines, pipelines and infrastructure. This raises productive capacity and growth.
  • If the borrowing funds investment that earns more than the interest cost, the deficit is sustainable. Under the Pitchford thesis, a deficit caused by private firms borrowing to invest is not a concern because those firms make their own decisions.

Negative effects

  • Higher primary income deficit: more of Australian income is paid overseas, so national income is lower than GDP.
  • Vulnerability to a loss of confidence: a high ratio of foreign debt to GDP means a change in sentiment can raise interest rates or cause the dollar to depreciate sharply.
  • Exchange rate risk: a depreciation raises the cost of repaying debt in foreign currency. Most Australian foreign debt is hedged, which limits the risk.
  • Policy constraints: a large CAD can limit how far the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) and the government can stimulate demand.

Links to other parts of the course

  • Balance of payments: the CAD is the current account balance, and the financial account is its mirror image.
  • Exchange rates: a floating dollar adjusts to help restore balance (Figure 7).
  • Growth and inflation: faster growth can raise imports, and imported inflation passes through a lower dollar.
  • Policies: fiscal policy can lift national saving, and microeconomic policy can lift competitiveness.

Use it in an exam: quote the CAD in dollars and as a share of GDP, link the deficit to the net primary income balance and net foreign liabilities, and explain why the level matters for sustainability. Always evaluate: a deficit is a problem only when it is persistent and funds consumption rather than productive investment.
