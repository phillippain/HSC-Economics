What the balance of payments is

The balance of payments (BOP) is the record of all economic transactions between Australia and the rest of the world over a period of time. The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) publishes it every quarter. It has two parts: the current account and the capital and financial account.

Every transaction is entered as a credit or a debit.

  • Credits are money flowing into Australia, such as payment for exports. They are positive.
  • Debits are money flowing out of Australia, such as payment for imports. They are negative.

The current account

The current account records transactions that cannot be reversed: trade and income. It has four parts (Figure 2).

  • Net goods: exports of goods minus imports of goods.
  • Net services: exports of services, such as education and tourism, minus imports of services, such as Australians' travel overseas. Net goods plus net services is the balance on goods and services (BOGS).
  • Net primary income: earnings on investments and labour. Credits are interest, dividends and wages received from overseas. Debits are interest paid on foreign debt and dividends and profits paid to foreign owners.
  • Net secondary income: transfers with nothing given in return, such as foreign aid, gifts and money migrants send home.

Current account balance = net goods + net services + net primary income + net secondary income

A negative total is a current account deficit (CAD).

The capital and financial account

This account records transactions in assets and liabilities, which can be reversed later.

  • The capital account is small. It covers capital transfers, such as money migrants bring with them, and the sale of non-produced assets such as patents and trademarks.
  • The financial account records investment flows. Its categories are direct investment, portfolio investment, financial derivatives, other investment and reserve assets held by the Reserve Bank.

When foreigners invest in or lend to Australia, money flows in, so it is a credit. Australia usually runs a surplus on this account.

Links between the accounts

With a floating exchange rate the two accounts balance. A current account deficit means Australia spends more overseas than it earns, and the difference must be paid for by an inflow of foreign money:

Current account balance + capital and financial account balance = 0

In the published figures a balancing item called net errors and omissions covers any gap in measurement.

The second link runs through time (Figure 5). A financial account surplus today adds to Australia's foreign liabilities. Those liabilities must be serviced: debt earns interest for the lender and equity earns dividends for the owner. Both are debits on net primary income, so the inflow of capital today widens the current account deficit in later years.

The underlying cause is the saving and investment gap. Australia invests more than it saves, and foreign saving fills the gap.

Trends in the size and composition

Australia has run a current account deficit in most years. It has averaged about 3.6 per cent of GDP since 1989 and was more than 7 per cent of GDP in 2007. The largest part has nearly always been the net primary income deficit.

From 2020 to 2022 Australia recorded current account surpluses, which was highly unusual (Figure 3). Commodity prices were very high, so the balance on goods and services moved into a large surplus.

The deficit has returned since 2023 and is widening (Figure 4). In the June quarter of 2026 it was $27.2 billion. Net primary income was a deficit of $21.9 billion: Australia received about $30.7 billion in income from overseas but paid about $52.6 billion. Higher interest payments on foreign debt were the main reason debits rose. The balance on goods and services also moved into deficit as imports grew faster than exports.

What drives the trends

The syllabus names four influences (Figure 7).

  • International competitiveness: if Australian firms have lower costs, higher productivity or better quality than rivals, exports rise and imports fall. Higher inflation than trading partners, or an appreciation of the dollar, reduces competitiveness.
  • Terms of trade: higher export prices relative to import prices raise export income and improve the balance on goods and services. Australia's terms of trade depend mostly on world prices for iron ore, coal and gas.
  • International borrowing: more foreign debt means more interest paid overseas. Higher world interest rates raise the cost of servicing that debt.
  • Foreign investment: foreign ownership of Australian businesses means profits and dividends flow overseas. In a mining boom, higher profits for foreign-owned miners raise primary income debits.

Effects of these trends

  • Growing foreign liabilities: each deficit must be financed, which adds to net foreign debt or foreign ownership.
  • The exchange rate: credits create demand for Australian dollars and debits create supply (Figure 6). A wider deficit raises supply, which tends to depreciate the currency. A depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, which helps to correct the deficit.
  • Constraint on growth: strong growth draws in imports and widens the deficit, which can limit how fast the economy can grow.
  • Is it a problem? One view is that a deficit caused by private firms borrowing to invest will pay for itself, because the investment raises future output and exports. The risk is a loss of confidence by foreign lenders if the debt looks hard to service.

Calculating the balance of payments

You must be able to calculate the main components (Figure 8). Take care with signs: add credits and subtract debits. If you are given the current account balance, the capital and financial account balance is the same number with the opposite sign.
