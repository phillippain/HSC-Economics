Free trade and protection

Free trade means goods and services move between countries without barriers imposed by governments. Protection is any government action that gives local producers an advantage over foreign competitors. The main methods are tariffs (a tax on imports), subsidies (payments to local producers), quotas (limits on the volume of imports) and local content rules.

Australia's policies: from protection to free trade

Until the 1970s Australia protected its manufacturers with some of the highest tariffs in the developed world. The aim was to build local industry and jobs. The cost was high prices for consumers and firms that had little reason to become efficient.

Governments then cut protection in steps. Tariffs were cut by 25 per cent across the board in 1973, and phased cuts followed from 1988 and 1991. Import quotas were removed. The tariff on imported cars fell from 57.5 per cent in 1987 to 5 per cent in 2010 (Figure 3).

Protection is now very low.

  • The highest general tariff is 5 per cent, and more than 90 per cent of imports by value enter duty free.
  • From 1 July 2024 the government removed 457 nuisance tariffs that raised little revenue.
  • Tariffs raise about $1.9 billion a year, but cost firms between $1.4 billion and $4.1 billion to comply with (Figure 5). The Productivity Commission argues the rest should go.
  • Help for industry has moved from tariffs to the budget. Grants, tax concessions and subsidies were worth $16.8 billion in 2024-25, including support for local manufacturing and clean energy.

Why Australia reduced protection

A tariff raises the price of imports, so consumers pay more and buy less, and resources are held in industries that cannot compete (Figure 2). Removing it reverses these effects. Firms must compete with imports, so they lift productivity or close. Resources then move to industries where Australia has a comparative advantage, such as mining, agriculture and services (Figure 6).

Free trade agreements

A free trade agreement (FTA) removes or lowers barriers between the countries that sign it. Australia is a member of the World Trade Organization (WTO), which sets global trade rules, but WTO talks have stalled. Australia has turned to agreements with single partners and regions. It has 19 in force, covering about 78 per cent of its trade.

Bilateral agreements have two members.

  • China-Australia Free Trade Agreement (2015): removed tariffs on most Australian exports to its largest market, including beef, dairy, wine and resources.
  • Australia-United States Free Trade Agreement (2005): removed most tariffs on trade between the two countries.

Multilateral (regional) agreements have many members.

  • Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP, 2018): links Australia with Japan, Canada, Mexico, Vietnam and other Pacific economies. The United Kingdom has since joined.
  • Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP, 2022): fifteen members, including China, Japan, South Korea and the ten ASEAN countries. It is the world's largest trade agreement by the size of its members' economies.

FTAs open markets for exporters and lower import prices. Their main weakness is trade diversion: buying from a partner because its goods are tariff free, even when another country produces them more cheaply. The Free Trade Agreements page has more detail.

Implications for individuals, firms and governments

  • Individuals: consumers gain lower prices and more choice, which raises real incomes. Workers in industries that lose protection can face structural unemployment. Car making in Australia ended in 2017, and the job losses fell on particular regions.
  • Firms: exporters gain access to larger markets and cheaper inputs. Import-competing firms face more competition and must become more efficient, change what they make or close.
  • Governments: tariff revenue falls, and governments face pressure to help the workers and regions affected. In the long run a more productive economy raises growth and tax revenue.

Protection by other countries and trading blocs

Australia gains little if its trading partners keep their own barriers (Figure 8).

  • United States: since 2025 the United States has placed tariffs on almost all trading partners. In August 2026 most Australian goods faced a 12.5 per cent tariff, and steel and aluminium faced rates of up to 50 per cent. Beef and gold were exempt. This is despite the free trade agreement between the two countries.
  • China: from 2020 China placed tariffs and bans on Australian barley, wine, coal, beef and lobster during a political dispute. The restrictions were lifted by the end of 2024. They showed the risk of relying on one market.
  • European Union: a trading bloc that subsidises and protects its farmers, which limits Australian farm exports. It now also charges for the carbon content of some imports. Australia and the European Union concluded talks on a free trade agreement in March 2026.

The direct effect of foreign protection is lower export income for the industries targeted. The indirect effect can be larger. Trade wars slow world growth and reduce the demand of Australia's main customers, especially China, for commodities.
