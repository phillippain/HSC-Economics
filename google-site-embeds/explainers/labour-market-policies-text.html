What labour market policies do

Labour market policies are the rules and programs that shape how workers and employers are matched, how wages and conditions are set, and how skilled the workforce is. They are a form of microeconomic (supply-side) policy. Their objectives are lower unemployment, higher participation, higher productivity and fair pay, without pushing up inflation. They work on the labour market itself, rather than on aggregate demand.

Wage setting and industrial relations

Australia moved from centralised wage fixing to enterprise bargaining in the 1990s, so that pay could reflect productivity at the firm level. The Fair Work Act 2009 now sets the framework: a safety net of National Employment Standards, modern awards for each industry, and bargaining for enterprise agreements, all overseen by the Fair Work Commission. Recent changes include the 2022 Secure Jobs, Better Pay reforms, which widened multi-employer bargaining, and the Building Cooperative Workplaces Act 2026, which took effect on 7 July 2026 and eases the workload of the Commission.

Minimum wages

Each year the Fair Work Commission reviews minimum wages. On 2 June 2026 it lifted modern award minimum rates by 4.75 per cent and the national minimum wage by about 6 per cent, to $26.44 an hour ($1,004.90 a week), from 1 July 2026. This is the first time the weekly rate has passed $1,000. About 2.8 million employees, roughly 21 per cent of the workforce, are paid at an award minimum rate. Supporters say minimum wages protect low-paid workers and support demand. Critics say a wage floor above the market wage can reduce the number of jobs for low-skilled workers, particularly young people (Figure 3).

National and state systems

Australia has a national system under the Fair Work Act 2009. It covers most private-sector employees, because every state except Western Australia referred its industrial relations powers to the Commonwealth by 2010. State systems still cover most state and local government employees and, in Western Australia, people who work for sole traders and partnerships. In the national system the Fair Work Commission sets minimum wages and awards, approves agreements and resolves disputes, and the Fair Work Ombudsman checks that employers comply.

How pay and conditions are set

  • National Employment Standards: minimum conditions for every national-system employee, such as a 38-hour standard week, annual and personal leave, and notice of termination.
  • National minimum wage: set each year in the Annual Wage Review (see above).
  • Modern awards: more than 100 awards set minimum pay and conditions for an industry or occupation.
  • Enterprise agreements: bargained between an employer (or several employers) and employees. The Commission approves an agreement only if employees are better off overall than under the award.
  • Contracts for high-income earners: an employee paid above the high income threshold ($183,100 a year in 2025–26) can be given a guarantee of annual earnings instead of the award, and can negotiate an individual common-law contract. Those not covered by an award or agreement cannot bring an unfair dismissal claim.

Dispute resolution

Awards and agreements must include a procedure for settling disputes. When the parties cannot agree, the Fair Work Commission helps through conciliation and mediation, and in some cases arbitration, where it makes a binding decision, for example after an intractable bargaining declaration. During bargaining, employees can take protected industrial action after a secret ballot. Individual disputes such as unfair dismissal also go to the Commission, and the Ombudsman and the courts enforce the law.

Centralised, decentralised and individualised methods

  • Centralised (a tribunal sets awards and minimum wages): protects low-paid and weaker workers and keeps wage rises orderly, but is inflexible and ignores differences in productivity between firms.
  • Decentralised (enterprise bargaining): links pay to productivity and suits each workplace, but can widen pay gaps and favours workers with more bargaining power. Multi-employer bargaining, widened in 2022, moves partly back towards a central system.
  • Individualised (individual contracts, such as Australian Workplace Agreements from 1996 to 2008, and today's high-income contracts): the most flexible, but workers with little bargaining power can lose conditions.

Education, training and skills

Skills policy tackles structural unemployment by improving the match between workers and jobs. Free TAFE is now permanent under the Free TAFE Act 2025, with a $1.5 billion agreement with the states for more than 500,000 places. Apprenticeship incentives and national skills priorities aim to fill shortages in areas such as construction, care and clean energy. Higher skills raise labour productivity and so shift the LRAS curve to the right.

Employment services and participation

Workforce Australia is the government employment service that helps job seekers train, find work and meet mutual obligation requirements. Income support such as JobSeeker acts as a safety net, but must be designed so that it does not weaken the incentive to look for work. Other measures lift participation, such as childcare subsidies, which make it easier for parents to work, and skilled migration, which adds workers where there are shortages.

The labour market now

Unemployment rose to 4.7 per cent in September 2026, the highest in almost five years, although the trend rate was 4.6 per cent. The RBA says a rate between 4.5 and 5 per cent is needed to ease inflation pressure, so policy is balancing two aims: keeping people in work, and keeping wage growth consistent with the inflation target.

Evaluating labour market policies

  • Strengths: they lift productivity and participation, reduce structural unemployment, protect low-paid workers and can make wages respond to firm performance.
  • Limitations: skills programs take years to pay off and may train people for the wrong jobs. Higher minimum wages may cost some jobs and raise business costs. Flexible bargaining can widen pay differences, and rules can add compliance costs.
  • Policy mix: they do not fix a cyclical downturn, so they work alongside fiscal and monetary policy.
