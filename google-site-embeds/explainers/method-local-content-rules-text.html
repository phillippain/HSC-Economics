What local content rules are

A local content rule requires that a set share of a good or service be made in the home country. A firm must use a minimum share of local parts, workers or programs to sell in the market or to win a government contract. Local content rules do not tax imports as a tariff does; they guarantee local producers a share of the market.

How they work

The rule forces firms to buy more from local suppliers, so demand for the local product rises (D1 to D2 in Figure 2). Local output, prices and jobs are higher than they would be under free trade. The government collects no revenue, unlike a tariff; the cost is hidden in higher prices, higher costs for the firms that must comply, and less choice for consumers.

Australian examples

  • Streaming services. A law passed in November 2025 applies from 1 January 2026. Streaming services with more than one million Australian subscribers must invest in new Australian drama, children's, documentary, arts and educational programs: either 10 per cent of what they spend on programs for Australia, or 7.5 per cent of their Australian revenue. The rule is phased in over three years and will be reviewed after four.
  • Radio and television. Commercial radio must play a minimum share of Australian music (up to 25 per cent, depending on the station's format), and free-to-air television has Australian content quotas.
  • Government purchasing. Defence, infrastructure and renewable energy contracts often give weight to local content, for example locally made steel or local workers.
  • Rules of origin in free trade agreements set how much of a good must be made in member countries for it to get the lower tariff.

Evaluating local content rules

  • For: they support local industries and jobs, protect national culture (Australian stories on screen), and can build skills and supply chains for defence or clean energy.
  • Against: they raise costs for firms and prices for consumers, protect inefficient producers, and are hard to measure, so their true cost is hidden. Trading partners may treat them as discrimination: US technology companies have pushed for trade retaliation over Australia's rules on digital platforms.

Use it in an exam: treat a local content rule like a quota in reverse: it guarantees a share for local producers rather than limiting imports. Explain who gains (local producers and workers) and who pays (consumers and the firms that must comply), then judge the effect on efficiency.
