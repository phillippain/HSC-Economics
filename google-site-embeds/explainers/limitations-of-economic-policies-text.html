Why policies have limits

Governments use fiscal, monetary and microeconomic policies to pursue the economic objectives: strong and sustainable growth, low inflation, low unemployment, external stability, fair distribution of income and environmental sustainability. No policy achieves all of these at once, and no policy works exactly as planned. The limitations of economic policies explain why the policy mix changes over time and why governments rarely hit every target together.

Time lags

Policy takes time to work. There is a recognition lag (it takes time for the data to show that a problem exists), a decision lag (policy makers must agree on a response), an implementation lag (the change must be put in place) and an impact lag (households and firms take time to change their behaviour). Monetary policy changes can take a year or more to have their full effect on inflation. Fiscal policy has long decision and implementation lags, because spending must be legislated, planned and delivered. Microeconomic reforms work slowest of all. If the lags are long, the economy may already have changed by the time the policy takes effect, and the policy can worsen the cycle instead of smoothing it (Figure 3).

Conflicting objectives and trade-offs

Policies that help one objective often hurt another. The clearest example is the trade-off between inflation and unemployment. On 3 November 2026 the Reserve Bank raised the cash rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4.85 per cent, its fifth increase of the year, to bring inflation back to the 2 to 3 per cent target. Annual inflation was 4.3 per cent in September and underlying inflation 3.6 per cent. But unemployment had risen to 4.7 per cent, and three of the nine board members voted to hold, mainly because of the weaker jobs market. Higher interest rates reduce spending and inflation, but they also reduce output and jobs (Figure 5).

Shocks outside the government's control

Australia is a small open economy, so many influences come from overseas. Much of the rise in inflation in 2026 came from higher fuel prices, which are set in world markets. A rise in the cost of production like this shifts the SRAS curve to the left, so that prices rise while output falls (Figure 4). Interest rates cannot lower the world price of oil. Raising them to fight cost-push inflation can deepen the slowdown. The Government responded in the 2026–27 Budget by temporarily halving fuel excise, which eases the pain at the pump but costs revenue and is politically hard to reverse.

Limits of each type of policy

  • Fiscal policy: long lags, crowding out of private investment if the Government borrows heavily, and limits on how much debt is sustainable. Spending is easier to start than to stop, and election timing can drive decisions. Extra spending when the economy is near capacity adds to inflation and works against monetary policy.
  • Monetary policy: a blunt tool that works mainly through borrowers and the exchange rate. It affects groups unevenly: households with large mortgages bear more of the cost than savers or those who own their homes outright. Its effect depends on how households and firms expect inflation to behave.
  • Microeconomic policy: benefits are spread over many years and are hard to measure, while costs such as job losses in a protected industry fall quickly on a few people. Reform can be blocked by those who lose.

Uncertainty and imperfect information

Policy makers cannot see the economy clearly. Data are revised, and key measures cannot be observed directly. The RBA estimates the neutral cash rate, the rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy, at somewhere between about 3 and 4.4 per cent. This wide range shows how hard it is to judge whether policy is tight or loose. Forecasts can be wrong, and a policy based on a wrong forecast can miss the target.

Equity and the policy mix

Policies have distributional effects. Tax cuts, interest rate rises and subsidies each help some groups more than others, so the best policy for the economy as a whole may not be fair. Fiscal, monetary and microeconomic policy can also pull in different directions, which is why coordination matters. To judge a policy, ask how fast it works, which objective it sacrifices, who gains and who loses, and what outside events could disrupt it.
