4Australian superannuation funds now hold very large investments overseas. Explain how this could change net primary income over the next decade.

Show a model answer

Income earned on overseas assets (dividends and interest) is a credit on net primary income. As Australian investment abroad grows ($4.8 trillion in 2026, 88% of foreign investment in Australia), those credits grow and offset more of the interest and dividends paid overseas. Net foreign equity is already negative (Australians own more equity overseas than foreigners own here). If the trend continues, the income deficit shrinks as a share of GDP. It will not disappear while net foreign debt remains about half of GDP, and it widens when world interest rates rise.

Band 6 writing: the savings–investment gap

Explain how the gap between national saving and investment affects the size and composition of Australia's balance of payments. (10 marks)

Plan it with the scaffold, write it by hand in 35 minutes, then mark it against the points.

  1. Define. National saving, investment and the gap between them; link it to the current account in one sentence.
  2. The financial account. How the gap is filled: borrowing (debt) and foreign ownership (equity).
  3. Net primary income. How past inflows become interest and dividends paid overseas every year.
  4. What changes the gap. Superannuation, budget deficits, investment booms.
  5. Use the latest figures and finish by linking size and composition back to the question.
Show the marking points
  • A current account deficit equals the excess of investment over national saving; overseas savings fill the gap.
  • The inflow is a surplus on the capital and financial account: $83.0 billion in 2025–26, matching a current account deficit of $94.9 billion (with net errors and omissions of $11.9 billion).
  • Composition: borrowing adds to net foreign debt ($1,486 billion, 50.8% of GDP); the servicing cost is the net primary income deficit ($85.5 billion), about nine-tenths of the current account deficit.
  • Size: the gap widens in investment booms (the mining boom: record deficit of 6.7% of GDP in 2007–08) and when the budget is in deficit; it narrows when saving rises (surpluses 2019–20 to 2022–23).
  • Superannuation (guarantee 12% from 2025) has lifted saving; super funds' overseas assets earn income credits and made net foreign equity negative.
  • A clear cause-and-effect chain throughout: gap → inflow → liabilities → income outflow → larger deficit.
Show a Band 6 opening

A country that invests more than it saves must use other countries' savings, and the balance of payments is where that shows. Australia has done this for most of its history: a small population with large investment needs in mining, housing and infrastructure. The inflow appears as a surplus on the capital and financial account, and the cost of it appears every year afterwards as a deficit on net primary income.
