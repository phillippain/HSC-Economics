Why governments act

Pollution is a negative externality. Firms and households do not pay the full cost of the damage they cause, so the market produces too much of it. This is market failure. Governments step in so that growth today does not reduce living standards for future generations. This goal is called ecologically sustainable development.

National context: Australia's main tools

  • Targets: 43 per cent below 2005 levels by 2030, 62 to 70 per cent by 2035 and net zero by 2050.
  • Safeguard Mechanism: the largest industrial sites must cut emissions each year or buy credits. This puts a price on their pollution.
  • Renewable energy: a goal of 82 per cent renewable electricity by 2030, backed by government underwriting of new wind, solar and storage.
  • Regulation and spending: vehicle emission standards, and rebates for home batteries and efficient appliances.

Global context

  • Climate change is a global problem, so one country acting alone cannot fix it. Each has an incentive to let others bear the cost. This is the free rider problem.
  • Paris Agreement (2015): countries agreed to hold warming well below 2 degrees and to aim for 1.5 degrees. Each sets its own target and updates it every five years.
  • Trade partners are acting too. The European Union now charges for the carbon in some imports, which affects exporters.

The trade-off

Australia earns large export income from coal and gas. Cutting emissions means structural change: some regions lose jobs while new industries grow. Official projections in 2025 showed a 48 per cent cut by 2035 on current policies, so more action is needed to reach the target.

Use it in an exam: Define the market failure, name a policy and how it changes incentives, then weigh the environmental gain against the short-run cost to growth, jobs and prices.
