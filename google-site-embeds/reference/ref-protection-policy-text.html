From protection to free trade

Until the 1970s Australia protected its factories with some of the highest tariffs in the developed world. Governments then cut protection in steps: a 25 per cent across-the-board cut in 1973, phased cuts from 1988 and 1991, and the end of import quotas. The aim was to force firms to compete and to shift resources to industries where Australia has a comparative advantage.

Where protection stands now

  • Tariffs are low. From 1 July 2024 the government removed 457 nuisance tariffs that raised little revenue.
  • Tariffs raised $1.9 billion, but cost firms between $1.4 billion and $4.1 billion to comply with. The Productivity Commission argues the rest should go.
  • Help for industry has moved from tariffs to the budget: grants, tax concessions and subsidies, such as support for local manufacturing and clean energy.
  • The effective rate of assistance to manufacturing is about 4 per cent, far below past levels.

Effects on the economy

  • Gains: lower prices and more choice for consumers, cheaper inputs for firms, higher productivity, and stronger export industries.
  • Costs: structural unemployment as protected industries shrank. Car making ended in 2017. Job losses fell on particular regions and workers.

Use it in an exam: Explain the reasons for cutting protection, then weigh the long-run efficiency gains against the short-run structural costs. Note that industry help is now mostly paid through the budget.

Sources: Productivity Commission, Trade and Assistance Review 2024-25; DFAT, About free trade agreements.
