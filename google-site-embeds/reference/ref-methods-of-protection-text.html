What protection is

Protection is any government action that gives local producers an advantage over foreign producers. The syllabus names five methods. Each one helps a local industry, and each one has a cost that someone else pays.

The five methods

  • Tariff: a tax on an imported good. The price of the import rises, so buyers switch to the local product. Local output rises, imports fall and the government collects revenue.
  • Subsidy: a payment from the government to local producers. It lowers their costs, so they can supply more at the world price. Consumers do not pay a higher price, but taxpayers fund the payment.
  • Quota: a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. With fewer imports for sale the price rises, and local producers sell more. The government collects no revenue. Importers keep the extra revenue from the higher price.
  • Local content rule: a set share of a good or service must be made in the home country. Local producers are guaranteed part of the market.
  • Export incentive: a grant, cheap loan or tax break that helps local firms sell overseas. It protects by lowering the exporter's costs, not by blocking imports. Australia's Export Market Development Grants are an example.

Effects on the domestic economy

  • Protected industry: higher output, jobs and profits in the short run.
  • Consumers: higher prices and less choice under tariffs, quotas and local content rules.
  • Other firms: higher input costs, and the risk that trading partners retaliate against their exports.
  • Efficiency: resources stay in industries where the economy lacks comparative advantage, so productivity and living standards are lower in the long run.
  • Budget: tariffs raise revenue. Subsidies and export incentives cost revenue.

Effects on the global economy

  • World trade and world output are lower, because countries specialise less.
  • Exporters in other countries lose sales. Developing economies are hit hard when rich countries protect farming.
  • One country's protection can lead others to retaliate, as with the United States and China since 2018.

Use it in an exam: Name the method, explain how it changes price, local production and imports, then say who gains and who pays. Draw the tariff or subsidy diagram when the question allows.
