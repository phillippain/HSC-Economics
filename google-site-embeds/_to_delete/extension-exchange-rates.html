Fixed and managed rates in Asia

Australia floats, but several of its trading partners do not. Read the table, then answer.

EconomySystemHow it works
Hong KongFixed (a currency board)The Hong Kong dollar has been linked to the US dollar since 1983 and is kept within a narrow band of HK$7.75 to HK$7.85. The monetary authority buys or sells US dollars to hold it there, so Hong Kong interest rates follow US rates.
ChinaManaged (a daily band)The central bank sets a reference rate each morning and the renminbi may move 2% either side of it.
SingaporeManaged against a basketThe monetary authority steers the Singapore dollar against a trade-weighted basket inside a band it does not publish. It uses the exchange rate, not an interest rate, as its main tool against inflation.
JapanFloating, with occasional interventionThe yen floats, but the authorities have bought yen to slow sharp falls, as in 2022 and 2024.

1Explain why Hong Kong cannot run its own monetary policy.

Show a model answer

To hold the peg, the monetary authority must buy or sell Hong Kong dollars whenever the rate reaches the edge of the band. If Hong Kong interest rates were lower than US rates, savings would flow out, people would sell Hong Kong dollars and the peg would come under pressure. So interest rates have to follow US rates, whatever the state of Hong Kong's own economy. A fixed rate means giving up an independent monetary policy.

2Outline ONE advantage and ONE disadvantage for an Australian exporter of a trading partner having a fixed or managed rate.

Show a model answer

Advantage: less exchange rate risk against that currency, which makes prices and contracts easier to plan. Disadvantage: the partner's currency does not adjust to market forces, so it may be held lower than a market rate would be, making that country's goods more competitive against Australian producers; and all of the adjustment to a shock falls on the Australian dollar.

Band 6 writing: the dollar as a shock absorber

Evaluate the view that a floating exchange rate protects the Australian economy from external shocks. (10 marks)

Plan it with the scaffold, write it by hand in 35 minutes, then mark it against the points.

  1. Define and judge. A floating rate, an external shock, and your overall view in the opening.
  2. How it absorbs a negative shock. Commodity prices fall → the dollar falls → other exports gain. Use a diagram of the market for A$.
  3. How it absorbs a positive shock. In a boom the dollar rises, which holds down inflation.
  4. The limits. Volatility, imported inflation, firms that lose in each case, and sentiment-driven swings.
  5. Conclude with a judgement and one comparison with a fixed rate.
Show the marking points
  • A floating rate is set by demand and supply; Australia has floated since December 1983.
  • Negative shock: lower commodity prices reduce demand for A$; the depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, cushioning growth and jobs (2008–09: the dollar lost one-third of its value and Australia avoided recession).
  • Positive shock: during the mining boom the dollar rose to US$1.10 (2011), making imports cheaper and containing inflation while resources drew in labour and capital.
  • Monetary policy stays free to target inflation, unlike under a fixed rate, where interest rates must defend the currency.
  • Limits: a lower dollar raises import prices (a 2% fall in the TWI adds about 0.2 points to inflation); a high dollar hurt manufacturing, tourism and education; about 95% of trading is speculative, so the dollar can overshoot.
  • The Reserve Bank can smooth extreme moves but its reserves are small relative to daily trading.
  • Judgement: the float has protected output and jobs from external shocks better than a fixed rate would, at the cost of volatility and uneven effects across industries.
Show a Band 6 opening

Since the float in December 1983 the Australian dollar has acted as the economy's first line of defence against changes in the rest of the world. When commodity prices fall the dollar falls with them, spreading the adjustment across exporters and import-competing firms instead of leaving it to output and jobs. The protection is real but incomplete: the same movements raise import prices, and they can be driven by sentiment as much as by trade.
