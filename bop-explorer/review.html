Shocks to trace

Changes are in $ billion from a base of goods and services +15, primary income −18, secondary income −2.

Export prices rise (the terms of trade improve) (dollar: appreciates)

  • At first: goods and services +6, primary income -1. Each tonne exported earns more, so export credits rise and the balance on goods and services improves. Much of the mining industry is foreign owned, so higher profits mean larger dividend debits. More export income means more demand for the Australian dollar, so it appreciates.
  • Later: goods and services +3, primary income -2. The higher dollar makes other exports less competitive and imports cheaper, giving back part of the gain. Dividends paid overseas stay high while mining profits are high. The dollar stays high while export prices do.

A global downturn (dollar: depreciates)

  • At first: goods and services -6, primary income +1. Trading partners buy fewer exports and commodity prices fall, so export credits fall. Lower profits for foreign-owned exporters mean smaller dividend debits. Less demand for exports means less demand for the dollar, so it depreciates.
  • Later: goods and services -3, primary income +0. The lower dollar makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, which recovers part of the loss. A lower dollar raises the cost of servicing debt owed in foreign currencies, offsetting the lower dividends. The dollar stays low until world demand recovers.

The Australian dollar depreciates (dollar: depreciates)

  • At first: goods and services -1, primary income -1. Volumes take time to respond, but imports already cost more in Australian dollars, so the balance can worsen at first (the J-curve). Interest on debt owed in foreign currencies now costs more Australian dollars. This is the starting change.
  • Later: goods and services +4, primary income -1. Exports are cheaper for foreign buyers and imports are dearer, so export volumes rise and import volumes fall. Servicing foreign-currency debt remains more expensive. A better trade balance later supports the dollar.

Australian interest rates rise relative to overseas (dollar: appreciates)

  • At first: goods and services +0, primary income -2. Higher interest rates mean larger interest payments to overseas lenders. Capital inflow raises demand for the dollar, so it appreciates. Foreign savers move funds into Australia for the higher return, so financial account credits rise.
  • Later: goods and services -3, primary income -2. The higher dollar makes exports dearer and imports cheaper. Slower domestic spending reduces imports a little, but the dollar effect is larger here. Interest payments overseas stay higher. The dollar stays high while the rate gap lasts. The inflow continues while the interest rate gap lasts.

Strong growth in domestic spending (dollar: depreciates)

  • At first: goods and services -5, primary income +0. Households and firms buy more of everything, including imports, so import debits rise. More imports mean more Australian dollars supplied, which pushes the dollar down.
  • Later: goods and services -5, primary income -1. Imports stay high while spending is strong. The larger deficit was financed from overseas, so interest and dividend payments grow. Pressure on the dollar continues while imports are high.

A mining investment boom funded from overseas (dollar: appreciates)

  • At first: goods and services -4, primary income +0. The projects need imported machinery and equipment, so import debits rise. The investment inflow raises demand for the dollar. Foreign companies bring in funds to build mines and gas plants: direct investment credits rise.
  • Later: goods and services +7, primary income -4. The new mines begin exporting, so export credits rise strongly. The foreign owners are paid dividends on their investment. Higher export income supports the dollar. Once the projects are built the inflow of investment slows.

National saving rises (dollar: little change)

  • At first: goods and services +3, primary income +0. Higher saving means lower spending, including on imports. Less capital inflow lowers demand for the dollar, but fewer imports lower its supply: little net change. With more saving at home, Australia needs to borrow less from overseas, so financial account credits fall.
  • Later: goods and services +3, primary income +2. Lower spending on imports continues. With less borrowing, foreign liabilities grow more slowly and servicing costs fall. Little net change. Less new borrowing each year.

World interest rates rise (dollar: depreciates)

  • At first: goods and services +0, primary income -3. Australia is a net borrower, so higher world rates raise the interest paid on foreign debt. Funds are attracted overseas, lowering demand for the dollar.
  • Later: goods and services +2, primary income -3. The lower dollar makes exports more competitive and imports dearer. Debt servicing stays more expensive. The dollar stays lower while world rates are high.
